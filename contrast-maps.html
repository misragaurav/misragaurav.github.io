Contrast maps and the subtraction method

A difference is only as meaningful as the condition you subtract

17 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · Neuroimaging

Most of what imaging says about the brain comes from subtraction. Scan people while they do a task, scan them while they do a control task, and subtract. What is left is attributed to whatever differs between the two. The idea goes back to Donders, who in the 1860s subtracted simple from choice reaction times to estimate the time taken by a decision.

How a contrast map is built

In fMRI, each voxel’s signal is modelled as a sum of responses to the conditions of the experiment. A contrast is a weighted difference between those responses, for example “painful heat minus warm heat”. Its map shows where the difference is reliable across trials and people.

Three properties of the method matter more than any detail of the statistics:

  1. The control defines the meaning. “Pain minus rest” includes everything about having a stimulus on the skin, paying attention, and preparing to rate. “Pain minus equally intense non-painful heat” isolates far less, and far more specifically.
  2. Pure insertion is an assumption. Subtraction assumes that adding a component to a task does not change the other components. It is often false.
  3. Overlap is not identity. Two contrasts lighting up the same region does not mean the region does the same thing in both.

In a 2015 Cerebral Cortex paper we used contrasts like these to show that motor control and pain processing engage overlapping parts of the human midcingulate cortex.1 Whether overlap reflects a shared function or two intermixed populations is a question the contrast alone cannot settle; it takes designs that cross the factors.

In language models: the same method, with the same trap

A concept vector is a contrast map. The model reads “Tell me about bread.”, its residual-stream activations are recorded, and the mean activation for a set of baseline words is subtracted. What is left is taken to be “bread”. The emotion vectors in project 4 are built the same way: activity for one emotion minus the average over all emotions.

Project 1 fell into the first trap above, and the way out is instructive. My baseline was 50 plain, singular names of everyday objects; my concept list was deliberately mixed. Because the two lists differed systematically, every concept vector contained, besides its own concept, the same extra component: the average concept word minus the average baseline word. That shared component made up 37% of the vectors’ squared length. With the published lists, which are mixed in the same way on both sides, it made up 1%. A detection-like pattern I had found depended on it. Subtracting my baseline from the published concept words restored the component and about half of the pattern. I first labelled the component “not a household object”, but my concept list held household items too and they carried it just as much; what it means is still unknown. Knowing where a confound comes from does not require knowing what it is.

The general rule carries over unchanged from imaging: before interpreting a difference vector, measure what all your difference vectors have in common. The mean of the vectors, and how similar they are to one another, takes one line of code. If every concept vector points partly the same way, part of every result is about the baseline, not the concept.
