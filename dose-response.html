Dose-response and the psychometric function

One condition is an anecdote; a curve across doses is a measurement

3 min read · Psychophysics

A single comparison, stimulus against no stimulus, tells you that something happened. A series of intensities tells you how it happened: where the response begins, how steeply it rises, where it saturates, and whether something other than the stimulus is lifting the whole curve. Pharmacology calls this a dose-response curve; psychophysics calls it a psychometric function. The logic is the same.

The method of constant stimuli

Choose a handful of intensities spanning “never detected” to “always detected”. Present each many times in random order, and plot the proportion of “yes” at each. Fit a sigmoid. Three numbers summarise it:

  • Threshold: the intensity at which the response reaches half its range. A more sensitive observer has a lower threshold, so the curve slides left.
  • Slope: how sharply the response changes around threshold.
  • Floor and ceiling: the response at zero intensity (the false-alarm rate, from catch trials) and at the highest intensity (one minus the lapse rate).

Top: higher sensitivity moves the curve sideways (lower threshold). Bottom: a readier “yes” lifts the curve, most visibly at zero dose, where it shows up as false alarms. A single high-intensity condition cannot tell these two apart; the full curve can.

This is why the curve matters for the sensitivity-versus-bias question on the signal detection page. A change in bias raises the floor; a change in sensitivity shifts the curve. Both raise the response at a single middling dose.

In my pain work

In a 2014 PLoS ONE study participants squeezed at 5%, 25% or 50% of their maximum voluntary force while a thermode heated the other hand.1 Pain relief grew with force (0.10, 0.32 and 0.62 points on a 0 to 10 scale), but only the 50% condition was significant, and only with the hot stimulus: at a merely warm temperature nothing happened at any force. Two lessons carried over directly: you need enough dose to see anything, and you need something to modulate. Holding the noxious stimulus constant and varying something else in steps is what lets a change in rating be attributed to the manipulation and not to the heat. The same design, a fixed probe and a graded intervention, is the backbone of my language-model projects.

In language models

Activation steering is a dose-response experiment that is rarely run as one. A direction vector is added to the model’s residual stream at some strength, and its effect on behaviour is reported, often at a single strength that “worked”.

  • In the introspection project the injection strength was the dose: every effect was measured at several strengths and judged only where the model still put most of its probability on “Yes” or “No” (above that, the model stops answering the question at all, which is the language-model version of a lapse).
  • In the emotion-readouts project a “distress” direction was added at four doses while the model answered neutral questions. The rated pleasantness of its answers fell in a clean, graded way: −0.3, −0.9, −1.3 and −2.0 points. A random direction of the same length did little at low doses, then broke the text (11 of 12 answers collapsed at the top dose), and broken text is rated unpleasant. Without the sham, the curve would have looked like dose-dependent distress induction. Compared with the sham dose for dose, the distress direction did more at only one dose, the highest before any answer broke (about one point more negative, by the stronger of two raters). The specific effect lives in a narrow window; most of the slope is what any large enough push does.
  • The same experiment showed a therapeutic window: directions the model uses in ordinary reading kept the text fluent at doses where the random direction destroyed it. The usable range of a dose depends on what is stimulated, not only on how hard, as anyone who has dosed tDCS knows.

What a dose-response design buys. It shows whether an effect is graded or all-or-none, separates a specific effect from a generic one that appears at the same intensity with any stimulus, and finds the window between “too weak to matter” and “strong enough to break everything”, which is where the interesting claims live.
