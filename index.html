Two ways to read emotion inside a language model

Do they agree, and does a “distress” reading respond to distress or to anything intense?

Two published instruments read emotion from a model's internal activations. I rebuilt both on Gemma 3 4B and tested them the way a pain signature is tested: for agreement, and for specificity against salience. Then I rebuilt the stimuli from identical words so that vocabulary could not do the work. The instruments read meaning, but which one looked specific reversed, and a clean steering dose-response was mostly matched by a random-direction sham.

Read the project · Full report · 640 passages, four raters, steering at four doses

I spent four years measuring pain in people with EEG and fMRI, and the hardest part was never the recording. It was knowing what a signal meant: pain or salience, perception or expectation, the experience or only the report. Language models now raise the same questions about their own internal states, with complete access to the system and no agreement about whether there is anything inside to report. This site collects my experiments on those questions, the measurement ideas they rest on, and the work I did before.

  • Two ways to read emotion inside a language model: eighteen models4 min read

    I ran my emotion-readout tests, built on one model, on eighteen small open models. The two published instruments agree only moderately in all of them, almost no distress reading is specific, and with the words held fixed the meaning is still readable. Steering with the distress direction does more than a random push of the same size in every model on average, but only by a little.

Concepts

Ideas from psychophysics, psychology and neuroimaging, and what they mean for language-model research.

  • Signal detection theoryPsychophysics3 min read

    Every yes/no report mixes two things, sensitivity and bias. Signal detection theory separates them, which is why it is the first tool to reach for when a model says it notices something.

  • Dose-response and the psychometric functionPsychophysics3 min read

    Vary the intensity of a stimulus or an intervention in steps and plot the response. Thresholds, slopes and ceilings each answer a different question, and a curve can tell sensitivity from bias where a single point cannot.

  • Contrast maps and the subtraction methodNeuroimaging3 min read

    Brain maps and concept vectors are both built by subtraction, condition minus control. Choosing the control is choosing what the map means, and a careless control can manufacture a finding.

  • Salience, and why it haunts EEG researchEEG3 min read

    Much of the brain's response to a painful stimulus is also produced by any sudden, attention-grabbing event. Salience controls are how pain research learned to tell the two apart, and how a distress probe in a language model should be tested.

  • Validity, specificity, and the multitrait-multimethod testMeasurement2 min read

    An instrument should agree with other instruments for the same trait, disagree with instruments for different traits, and not respond to things that merely travel with the trait. Psychology has tested this since 1959; interpretability probes rarely are.
