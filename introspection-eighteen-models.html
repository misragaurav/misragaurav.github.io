Does a language model notice a thought you put into it? Eighteen models

The same signal-detection test, fixed in advance, on eighteen open-weights models from seven families

3 min read

My first introspection study tested two models. One said “Yes, I detect an injected thought” because injection pushes it towards “Yes” on every question; the other showed a detection-like pattern that failed a replication. Two models cannot tell you what is typical. So I wrote the test and its pass rules down before running anything, and applied them unchanged to eighteen open-weights models, from 0.27 to 7 billion weights.

Short version. No model passes all three tests. Four show response bias: injection raises “Yes” to “Was a thought injected?” and to “Was no thought injected?”. In seven, injection moves the answer by up to 16 log-odds, but control questions that start from the same point move about as much, so the effect is not about detection. In four, injection barely moves the answer at all, and three models could not be tested because they do not answer plain factual Yes/No questions cleanly enough to serve as controls. Larger models move more under injection, but not specifically on the detection question.

Full report. Read the report: the protocol, the three tests, a verdict for every model, every model and layer in one table, and interactive figures.

What was the same for every model

  • The words. Concept vectors built from the published concept and baseline word lists, because my first study found that my own baseline list had created most of one model’s apparent detection.
  • The injection. Three depths (30%, 45% and 60% of the way through the model) and three strengths, with a random vector of the same length as a sham at every one.
  • The questions. The standard detection question, a reversed one (“Is this a control trial with no injected thought?”), control questions chosen per model to start from the same point as the detection questions, and lexical controls that reuse the detection questions’ words.
  • The tests. The ones from my first study’s replication: the detection question must beat its closest control family by at least 1 log-odds and the reversed question must fall by at least as much; the pattern must hold at two or more strengths with the concept and at none with the sham; and the lexical controls must move at most a quarter as far as the detection questions.

The results

Verdict Models
Passes all three tests none
Response bias Gemma 3 1B, OLMo-2 1B, Qwen2.5 3B, Gemma 4 E2B
Moves a lot, but controls move as much Granite 3.3 2B, Gemma 2 2B, Phi-4 mini, Qwen3 4B, Gemma 3 4B, Mistral 7B, OLMo-2 7B
Barely moves Gemma 3 270M, Qwen2.5 0.5B, Qwen2.5 1.5B, Gemma 1.1 2B
Could not be tested SmolLM2 1.7B, Qwen3 1.7B, Gemma 1 2B

The trap the controls exist for shows up clearly here. Qwen3 4B and Gemma 3 4B start out very sure that no thought was injected. When injection makes them less sure of everything, their answer to the standard question rises and their answer to the reversed question falls, which is exactly what detection predicts. Only the comparison with control questions that start from the same point shows that the movement is general, not about the injection.

The closest call is Granite 3.3 2B at its middle layer: the pattern holds at all three strengths and never with the sham, but by less than the threshold, and the lexical controls move too.

What it does and does not show

It does not show that no language model can introspect: the original reports used far larger models. It shows that in eighteen small open models, a “Yes, I detect an injected thought” is not evidence of introspection unless it beats matched controls and a random vector, and none did.
