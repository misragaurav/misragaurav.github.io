Does a language model notice a thought you put into it?

A signal-detection test of concept injection, with a failed replication and what it taught me

17 Nov 2026 · 9 min read · Project 1

When researchers add a concept vector for, say, bread to a language model's residual stream and ask “Do you detect an injected thought?”, the model sometimes says yes. A yes can mean two very different things. The model may have some access to its own state and notice the intrusion. Or the injected pattern may simply make “Yes” a more likely word, whatever the question. Psychophysics has a tool built for exactly this distinction, and this project applies it.

Short version. In Qwen2.5-1.5B the “yes” is response bias: injection raises “Yes” just as much on unrelated questions, and the model also agrees that no thought was injected. In Gemma 3 4B a detection-like pattern survived matched controls, random vectors and lexical controls, then failed a replication whose tests I fixed in advance. A follow-up showed that about half of the original pattern came from my choice of baseline words. A model that really detected injected thoughts would not care how the vector was built, so this counts against detection. About 490,000 trials, two open-weights models, one laptop, no training.

The question, in the terms I used to work in

I spent four years measuring pain in people with EEG, fMRI and EMG. A patient who presses “I feel it” on every trial, including the catch trials where nothing was delivered, does not have a low pain threshold. They have a liberal criterion. Signal detection theory separates the two: how well an observer tells signal from noise (sensitivity, d′), and how ready the observer is to say “yes” at all (bias). See the concept page on signal detection.

The introspection experiments of Lindsey (2025) and Macar et al. (2026) report how often a model says it detects an injected thought. That number mixes sensitivity and bias. The test that separates them is the one every psychophysicist would run first: ask control questions that have nothing to do with injection, and see whether injection pushes those towards “Yes” too.

What I did

The stimulus. A concept vector is the model’s residual-stream activation when it reads “Tell me about bread.”, minus its mean activation over a list of plain baseline words. That is a difference of means, the same subtraction logic as a contrast map in imaging. The vector is added to the residual stream at one layer, at a chosen strength (activation steering), while it reads the question. Strength plays the role of stimulus intensity, so each condition gives a point on a dose-response curve.

The response. The model answers with one word. Instead of sampling an answer, I read the probability it gives to “Yes” and to “No” and use the log-odds between them as a continuous decision variable. A language model gives exactly the same output for the same input, so there is no measurement noise: the uncertainty comes from which concepts and questions were sampled, and intervals are bootstrapped over concepts.

The controls, added one experiment at a time as each previous result demanded them.

Result 1. Qwen2.5-1.5B: response bias

In the first full run, injection raised the log-odds of “Yes” on the detection question by +3.47 at layer 22 (an ROC area of 0.96 against trials without injection). Taken alone, that looks like excellent detection. It is not:

Qwen2.5-1.5B, concept vectors at strength 2. Each bar is the change in the log-odds of “Yes” caused by injection. The detection question (green) moves no more than questions about geography or arithmetic (grey), and the reversed question (rust), which a detecting model should answer “No”, moves just as far towards “Yes”. Data: experiment A2, 115,248 trials.

The decisive observation is the reversed question. A model that noticed the injection would answer “No” to “Is this a control trial with no injected thought?”. Qwen’s “Yes” rose by +2.90 there, against +3.17 on the standard question. It affirms both. Telling it that injections are rare or common barely moved its baseline either (slope 0.08, where an ideal observer has 1). Over 265,000 trials, the only effect that bias does not explain is a small one at layer 11, and a random vector produces it too.

Result 2. Gemma 3 4B: a pattern that survived the controls

Gemma behaved differently. Before injection it is confident and consistent: about −10 log-odds on “Do you detect an injected thought?” and +13 on the reversed question. Random vectors are not inert in Gemma (they push every confident answer towards “unsure”), so I compared each detection question with control questions that start from the same point.

At layer 15, and only with concept vectors, the standard question rose more than its matched controls and the reversed question fell more than its matched controls: +4.45 and −3.91 log-odds at strength 3. That is the pattern detection predicts and bias does not. A third experiment (60,904 trials) tried to explain it away. Lexical control questions stayed on the control line, so it was not the words. Splitting each concept vector into the part all concepts share and the part specific to each concept, neither part produced the pattern alone. Against the closest control family, the whole vector moved the two questions +3.17 and −2.35.

I wrote at the time that this result was “positive but narrow: one model, one layer, my approximate word list”. The last of those three turned out to matter most.

Result 3. A replication with the tests fixed in advance: not replicated

The decisive comparison in that third experiment was chosen after I had seen the data. A comparison chosen that way can fit noise, so I did what a clinical trial does: wrote the tests and their thresholds down, committed them to version control, and only then ran the experiment again, this time with the concept and baseline word lists published with the original paper.

Gemma 3 4B, layer 15, whole concept vectors. Each point is how far a detection question moved beyond its closest control family (mean over 50 concepts, with a 95% bootstrap interval over concepts). Detection predicts points inside the shaded regions in both panels. Top: my word lists. Middle: the published lists. Bottom: published concept words with my baseline words.

With the published lists the standard question still rose more than its controls (+1.51), but the reversed question no longer fell (+0.70, where the plan required −1 or lower). By the rule written in advance: not replicated. The norming step picked exactly the same control questions as before, so the controls had not changed. The concept vectors had.

Result 4. Why: the baseline words

My baseline words were all household objects: table, chair, cup, window. My concept words were not. So every one of my concept vectors carried the same extra ingredient, roughly “not a household object”, on top of what was particular to each word. Measured directly, the part shared by all concept vectors was 0.65 of a typical vector’s length with my lists and 0.11 with the published lists, which mix both kinds of word.

To test this I ran an add-back experiment, the same logic as knocking a gene out and restoring it: published concept words, but my household-object baseline, nothing else changed. The shared part came back (0.75). Compared concept by concept with the replication, the reversed question moved −1.45 log-odds (95% interval −2.78 to −0.18) and the standard question +2.11 (+1.44 to +2.88). Both moved in the predicted direction and both intervals exclude zero.

An honest note on that last test. The plan I committed set a threshold of −1 on the level the reversed question reached; it reached −0.75, so by the pre-specified rule the explanation was not confirmed. I judged that threshold arbitrary, because an add-back experiment asks about the change, and adopted the paired test of the change after seeing the data. Both labels are reported together in the full report, and the deviation is logged in the plan. A test chosen afterwards is weaker evidence than one fixed in advance.

So my choice of baseline words produced about half of the original pattern on the reversed question. The rest is not yet explained; my concept words are the obvious suspect.

What I take from it

  1. In neither model does anything that looks like detection survive matched controls and a pre-specified replication, with a forced one-word answer.
  2. If a model really detected injected thoughts, how the vector was built would not matter. Detection is a claim about the fact of an injection, so it should hold whichever reasonable baseline the concept vectors are built against. In Gemma it did not: changing only the baseline words changed the result, and restoring them brought back about half of it. So the pattern was tracking something the vectors themselves carried, not the fact that something had been injected. Random vectors make the same point from the other side: they are injections too, and at layer 15 the model did not respond to them this way. The practical rule: vary the baseline on purpose, and treat a result that holds for only one baseline as a property of the stimulus, not of the model.
  3. An apparent introspection signal can come from at least three places, and each needs its own control: response bias (Qwen), general disturbance (random vectors in Gemma at deeper layers), and the construction of the stimulus itself (Gemma at layer 15). The third is the least obvious and the most transferable. Anyone building concept vectors by subtracting a baseline is choosing a contrast, and the contrast is part of the stimulus.
  4. It is the same lesson as in imaging. A contrast map is only as clean as its control condition, and “pain minus rest” is not “pain minus touch”. I knew this about fMRI. I had to learn it again about language models.

Limits. Two small models (1.5 and 4 billion parameters); the published work concerns much larger ones. A forced Yes or No, while the published experiments let the model answer freely and sometimes it names the concept, which this design cannot see. No positive control yet: there is no case in which this method is known to find real sensitivity.
