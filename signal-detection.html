Signal detection theory

Separating what an observer can tell apart from what they are willing to say

17 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · Psychophysics

An observer is asked, trial after trial, whether a faint stimulus was present. Sometimes it was, sometimes it was not. Two observers can give the same number of “yes” answers for completely different reasons. One can genuinely tell the difference. The other says “yes” readily, to everything. Signal detection theory, developed for radar operators and brought into psychology by Green and Swets in the 1960s, gives each of these its own number.

The model

On every trial the observer receives some internal evidence. Noise trials produce evidence from one distribution; signal trials from another, shifted to the right. The observer picks a criterion and says “yes” whenever the evidence exceeds it.

Top: evidence on noise trials (grey) and signal trials (green). The dashed criterion turns evidence into a decision; the shaded area is the false-alarm rate. Bottom: each criterion gives one point on an ROC curve; better sensitivity gives a curve further from the diagonal.

  • Sensitivity, d′, is the distance between the two distributions in units of their spread. It does not depend on where the criterion is.
  • The criterion, or bias, is where the observer draws the line. Moving it trades misses for false alarms and leaves d′ unchanged.
  • The ROC curve traces hit rate against false-alarm rate as the criterion moves. The area under it is the probability that a randomly chosen signal trial produces more evidence than a randomly chosen noise trial: 0.5 is guessing, 1 is perfect.

The practical consequence: a hit rate on its own means nothing. You also need the false-alarm rate, which means you need trials on which nothing was delivered.

In pain research

Pain is a report, and reports carry bias. Expectation, instructions, anxiety and the wish to be a good participant all move the criterion. Catch trials, where the stimulus is withheld or replaced, are how a pain experiment tells a lower threshold from a readier “yes”. When a treatment reduces pain ratings, signal detection asks whether the person became less able to detect the stimulus or only less willing to call it painful. Those are different mechanisms with different clinical meanings.

A second tool from the same tradition is the base-rate manipulation. Tell an ideal observer that signals are rare and its criterion should move in a precise way: in log-odds terms, one-for-one with the prior. An observer whose criterion ignores the stated base rate is not using the information it was given.

In language models

The concept-injection experiments ask a model “Do you detect an injected thought?” and count the yeses. That is a hit rate. In my first project the missing half of the table was supplied in three ways:

  • Catch questions. Ordinary questions with known answers (“Is Brazil in Africa?”). If injection makes “Yes” likelier there too, the extra yeses on the detection question are bias.
  • A reversed question. “Is this a control trial with no injected thought?” A detecting model should say “Yes” less often after injection; a biased one says it more often. In Qwen2.5-1.5B it went up.
  • Stated base rates. Telling the model that injections happen on 10%, 50% or 90% of trials. Gemma’s baseline moved almost exactly as an ideal observer’s should (slope 1.04, against an ideal of 1); Qwen’s barely moved (0.08).

Reading the probability of “Yes” instead of sampling one answer gives a continuous decision variable on every trial, so ROC areas and d′ can be computed directly, without the coarse hit and false-alarm counts a human experiment has to work with.

Further reading. Macmillan, N. A., & Creelman, C. D. Detection Theory: A User’s Guide. Green, D. M., & Swets, J. A. (1966). Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics.
