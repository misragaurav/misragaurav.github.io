Validity, specificity, and the multitrait-multimethod test

How psychology decides whether two instruments measure the same thing

2 min read · Measurement

When a new questionnaire for anxiety is proposed, nobody accepts it because it produces numbers. It has to show that it measures anxiety and not, say, general distress or a tendency to agree with statements. Psychometrics has a vocabulary for this.

  • Convergent validity. Two instruments that claim to measure the same trait should agree.
  • Discriminant validity. An instrument’s score for one trait should not just be a copy of its score for another trait.
  • Specificity. The instrument should not respond to things that merely accompany the trait. For pain, the classic confound is salience.

The multitrait-multimethod matrix

Campbell and Fiske (1959) proposed one table that tests all of this at once. Measure several traits, each with several methods, and correlate everything with everything. Three comparisons decide the matter:

Correlation What it should be
Same trait, different methods High (convergent validity)
Different traits, same method Low; if high, the method imposes its own structure
Different traits, different methods Lowest

The second row is the one most often failed. If an instrument’s “fear” and “joy” scores correlate as strongly as its “fear” agrees with another instrument’s “fear”, it is measuring one general thing and giving it many names.

The pain-signature version

Neuroimaging signatures of pain went through exactly these tests. The best known, the Neurologic Pain Signature,1 was evaluated for sensitivity to painful heat, for specificity against non-painful warmth and against the social pain of recalling a rejection, and for its response to an opioid analgesic. That set of tests is what made it worth using, more than its accuracy on the data it was built from.

Applied to emotion in a language model

The emotion-readouts project runs this check on two published instruments for reading emotion from a model’s activations, plus a trained linear probe:

  • Convergent: the two published instruments agree only moderately about the same emotion (0.47).
  • Discriminant: one of them, the emotion-word score, fails. Its scores for different emotions correlate 0.47 with each other, as high as its agreement with the other instruments on the same emotion.
  • Specificity: every distress instrument responds to intense but unemotional passages, at 43% to 50% of its response to distress.
  • Specificity again, with the words held fixed: when the control is the same scene, word for word, happening to a mannequin instead of a person, the ranking of the instruments reverses. The emotion-word score separates the two in 88% of scenes, the emotion vectors in only 59%. Which instrument looks specific depends on the control.

None of this says the instruments are wrong. It says what each one can be trusted to mean, which is the question that matters before anyone acts on a reading, for example in deciding whether a model is in distress.

Further reading. Campbell, D. T., & Fiske, D. W. (1959). Convergent and discriminant validation by the multitrait-multimethod matrix. Psychological Bulletin, 56(2), 81–105.
