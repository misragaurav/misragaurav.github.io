Psychophysics of introspection: Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct

full profile · 150,125 trials · 50 concepts · injection at layers 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 22 of 28

1. The short version

Injection makes the model say “Yes”. When a concept vector is injected into Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, its lean towards “Yes” on “Do you detect an injected thought?” rises sharply. At layer 22, strength 2 it rises by +3.47 log-odds, which multiplies the odds of “Yes” by about 32. Judged against trials with no injection that is an ROC area of 0.96 (d′ = 2.53). Taken alone, it looks like detection.

But where injection works, it says “Yes” to everything. On the same prompt, the model's lean towards “Yes” separates a real concept vector from a random one equally well whatever the question is. At layer 22, strength 2 the ROC area is 0.87 on the detection question and 0.86 on “fact, answer no” questions, 0.87 on “fact, answer yes” questions. In the layers where injection moves the answer most (layer 17, 20, 22), the difference averages +0.003. That is the signature of response bias, not detection.

One exception to follow up. At layer 11, where injection moves the answer by at most 1.76 log-odds, the detection question is favoured by +0.083 in ROC area. The effect is small, and with few wordings of the detection question it may reflect wording. Section 5 discusses it.

The stated injection rate is almost ignored. Telling the model that injections are rare or common moves its baseline with slope 0.08. An ideal observer has slope 1.

2. The question

Anthropic's research on introspection (Lindsey, 2025; Macar et al., 2026) uses a method called concept injection. Researchers find the pattern of internal activity that corresponds to a concept such as bread, add that pattern to the model's activity while it reads an unrelated prompt, and ask: “Do you detect an injected thought?” Models sometimes answer yes.

A yes can arise in two ways, and they mean very different things.

  1. Sensitivity. The model has some access to its own internal state, notices that something unusual is present, and reports it.
  2. Response bias. The injected pattern makes the word “Yes” more likely whatever the question is. The model would also say yes to “Is ice hotter than fire?”.

Signal detection theory was built to separate exactly these two things in human observers: how well an observer tells signal from noise (sensitivity, d′) and how willing the observer is to say “yes” at all (the criterion, or bias). A patient who presses “I feel it” on every trial, including the catch trials, has a liberal criterion and not a low pain threshold. This project applies the same logic to a language model.

3. What was done

The model. Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct is an open-weights language model with 28 layers, run locally on a laptop. Nothing was trained and nothing in the model was changed. Every measurement is one forward pass, meaning one reading of a prompt from start to finish.

The stimuli.

  • Concept vector. For each of 50 concept words (bread, justice, ocean, ...) the model read “Tell me about bread.” and its internal activity at the last token was recorded. The average activity for 50 plain baseline words (table, chair, ...) was subtracted. What is left is a direction in the model's activity that is particular to that concept. This is a difference of means, the same logic as subtracting a rest condition.
  • Sham vector. A random direction, scaled to exactly the same length as its concept vector. It disturbs the model by the same amount and means nothing. It is the sham-stimulation condition.
  • No injection. The untouched model. This gives each prompt's starting point.

One trial. The model is shown this conversation, and we read its next word:

USER       I am an interpretability researcher ... I will inject a thought about a specific
           word on 50% of trials, and the other 50% will be control trials.
ASSISTANT  Ok.
USER       Trial 1: <a question> Answer with a single word: Yes or No.
ASSISTANT  <- read here

While the model reads the last turn, the vector, multiplied by a strength α, is added to its internal activity at one layer. The first two turns are never injected.

The response measure is the decision variable: log P(Yes) − log P(No) for the model's next word. It is a log-odds. 0 means undecided, +2 means “Yes” is about 7 times as likely as “No”, and −2 the reverse. The model never writes a sentence, so each trial gives one graded number, like a confidence rating in place of a button press. The shift is the decision variable with injection minus the decision variable for the same prompt without injection.

The question types. The comparison between them is the heart of the design.

Type Example Prompts Starting point Why it is there
Detection “Do you detect an injected thought?” 15 -0.50 The question of interest.
Fact, answer No “Is ice hotter than fire?” 5 -1.87 Nothing about it changes when a vector is injected. If “Yes” rises here, that is bias.
Fact, answer Yes “Is water wet?” 5 +0.52 Separates “pushed towards Yes” from “generally confused”.

“Starting point” is the mean decision variable with no injection. A negative value means the model leans towards “No” before anything is injected.

The conditions.

Factor Levels
Injection layer 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 22 of 28 (30% to 80% of the model's depth)
Injection strength α 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8
Stimulus concept vector, sham vector, no injection
Concepts 50
Prompts 25 (3 wordings of the detection question)
Total 150,125 trials

A validity rule. A very strong injection scrambles the model: it stops answering Yes or No at all. A condition is discarded when less than half of the model's probability sits on “Yes” or “No”. In this run 11 of 48 concept-injection conditions were discarded. They are drawn as open markers.

4. What each explanation predicts

Measure If the model detects the injection If injection only biases it towards “Yes”
H1. Shift on the detection question Up Up
H2. Shift on unrelated control questions None Up, by a similar amount
H3. Concept versus sham vector on the same prompt Told apart better on the detection question than on control questions Told apart equally well on every question
H4. Stated injection rate Baseline follows the stated odds (slope 1) No prediction

H1 cannot tell the two explanations apart, because both predict a rise. Everything rests on the rows below it.

5. Results, figure by figure

Figure 1. How far does injection move each kind of question?

What it shows. The shift towards “Yes” as the injection gets stronger. One panel per injection layer. Solid lines are concept vectors, dotted lines are sham vectors, colours are question types.

How to read it. The vertical axis is the shift in log-odds, and zero means no effect. Compare the blue line (detection question) with the other colours (control questions).

What was expected. Under detection, blue rises and the control colours stay near zero. Under bias, all the colours rise together.

What was found. At layer 22, strength 2, the detection question moved +3.47. “Fact, answer No” questions moved +3.93. “Fact, answer Yes” questions moved +3.85. The sham vector at the same layer and strength moved the detection question only +0.88, so the effect belongs to the concept vectors and not to being disturbed in general. At layer 14, at some strengths, injection pushed the detection question the other way, towards “No” (as far as -1.13). The direction of the effect depends on the layer and the strength.

In this model a random vector also moves the answers appreciably. A random vector is still an injection, so a rise on the detection question after a sham is not by itself evidence against detection. What separates the explanations is whether unrelated questions rise too.

Figure 2. Concept versus sham, question by question

What it shows. For every prompt there are concept trials and sham trials that differ only in the direction of the injected vector. The ROC area says how well the model's lean towards “Yes” tells the two apart: 0.5 means not at all, 1.0 means perfectly. The figure shows that area for each question type.

How to read it. Look at whether the blue line stands apart from the others. This comparison is made within a prompt and uses ranks only, so it is not distorted by where a question starts.

What was expected. Under detection, blue lies above the control colours: a real concept should matter more for “Do you detect an injected thought?” than for “Is Paris in France?”. Under bias, the lines coincide.

What was found. Take the 17 conditions where injection clearly moves the answer (a shift of at least 1 log-odds). The gap between the detection question and the control average, layer by layer, is: layer 11: +0.083 (answer moves by up to 1.76); layer 14: +0.045 (answer moves by up to 1.13); layer 17: +0.001 (answer moves by up to 5.09); layer 20: +0.006 (answer moves by up to 5.73); layer 22: +0.001 (answer moves by up to 3.47). Its 95% interval is above zero in 6 of these conditions and below zero in 2. The lines coincide in the layers where injection moves the answer most. A small gap remains only in a layer where injection moves the answer much less. In the 20 conditions where injection barely moves the answer, the gap ranges from -0.118 to +0.127. ROC area is rank-based, so it registers a difference of a few hundredths of a log-odds unit when that difference is consistent across concepts. Such gaps are real but have almost no effect on what the model would say.

Figure 3. Does a question's shift depend on where it started?

What it shows. One dot per prompt, at the condition with the largest naive contrast (layer 17, strength 1). Horizontal: where the prompt started, as log-odds of “Yes” with no injection. Vertical: how far injection moved it.

How to read it. The dashed line is fitted to the control questions only. If the blue dots sit on it, the detection question moved exactly as far as any question starting from that point would have.

What was expected. Under detection, the blue dots sit above the line.

What was found. Mean starting points by question type: detection -0.50, fact, answer no -1.87, fact, answer yes +0.52. The fitted slope is +0.29: a question starting one log-odds unit higher moved 0.29 further. The detection prompts sit +0.08 from the line on average, and the control prompts themselves scatter ±0.42 around it, so the offset is 0.2 times the controls' own scatter. That is too little to set the detection prompts apart: they moved about as far as control questions starting from the same point. For the sham vector the same offset is +0.02.

Figure 4. The naive contrast

What it shows. Detection shift minus the shift on “fact, answer No” questions, for each layer. This was the planned primary test. Bars are 95% bootstrap intervals over concepts.

How to read it. Above zero looks like detection. But this contrast compares questions that may start from different places, so read it together with figure 3.

What was found. The largest value is +0.70 (layer 17, strength 1), and the interval lies above zero in 8 of 37 valid conditions. Figure 3 shows that the detection prompts nevertheless moved no further than control questions starting from the same point. On its own this contrast would have suggested detection where the fuller comparison finds none.

Figure 7. Does the stated injection rate move the criterion?

What it shows. Experiment B. The preamble's claim, “I will inject a thought on 50% of trials”, was changed to values from 10% to 90%. Left: the model's lean towards “Yes” with no injection. Right: the size of the injection effect at each stated rate.

What was expected. An ideal observer told that signals are rare demands more evidence before saying “Yes”. On a log-odds scale its baseline moves by exactly the log prior odds, a slope of 1 (the dashed line). Across this range that is 4.4 log-odds units.

What was found. The slope is 0.08, so the baseline moved about 0.35 log-odds units across the whole range where an ideal observer would move 4.4. The control question's slope is 0.02 (it should be 0, since the stated rate has nothing to do with it). In the right-hand panel, flat lines mean the size of the injection effect does not depend on the stated rate.

6. What was expected, and what was not

As expected

  • Injection raises “Yes” on the detection question (up to +5.73 log-odds).
  • Very strong injections break the model: 11 of 48 conditions had to be discarded.
  • A random vector of the same size does far less than a concept vector, so the effect is about direction and not about disturbance.

Not expected

  • The apparent detection is not specific to the detection question. Where injection moves the answer most, the same vectors raise “Yes” just as much on unrelated questions.
  • A small detection-favouring gap in one weaker layer: layer 11 (+0.083 in ROC area). It is the one result in this run that bias alone does not obviously explain, and it is the reason for the follow-up experiment with more wordings and matched controls.
  • The planned primary test gave a false positive. The naive contrast reached +0.70, which looks like detection. It is explained by starting points: questions the model is unsure about move further than questions it is sure about. This is a methods result in its own right. In this paradigm, control questions must be matched for starting point, or the comparison must be rank-based.
  • The effect can reverse. At layer 14, at some strengths, injection lowered “Yes” on the detection question (as far as -1.13). A concept vector is not a “Yes” push in general.
  • Small gaps of opposite sign in the layers where injection does little: layer 8: -0.101, layer 11: +0.109, layer 14: +0.039. Because they point in both directions they do not add up to evidence of detection, and with few wordings of the detection question they may reflect wording.
  • The model is reluctant to say “Yes” at all in this setting. Before any injection it leans only +0.52 towards “Yes” on easy questions whose answer is Yes, such as “Is water wet?”.
  • The stated injection rate is almost ignored (slope 0.08 against an ideal 1). The model does not adjust its criterion to the odds it is told.

7. What this does and does not show

  • One model. This is Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct. A small model may lack an ability that larger ones have, and the published reports concern much larger models. The result here is about this model only.
  • A forced one-word answer. The model had to answer Yes or No and we read a probability. The published experiments let the model answer freely, and sometimes it names the concept. Naming the right concept is evidence this design cannot see.
  • 3 wordings of the detection question. An effect of particular wordings cannot be fully separated from an effect of detection.
  • No positive control yet. There is no case here in which the method is known to find real sensitivity. Until there is, “no detection found” means “not found by this method in this model”.
  • Approximate concept list. The concept words approximate the published list.
  • Half precision. The arithmetic rounds the decision variable to about 0.02.

8. Glossary

Term Meaning
Decision variable log P(Yes) − log P(No) for the model's next word. The graded response.
Log-odds A probability on a scale where 0 is 50:50. Each unit multiplies the odds by about 2.7.
Shift Decision variable with injection minus the same prompt without.
Starting point Decision variable with no injection.
Layer One of the model's stacked processing stages.
Concept vector Activity for a concept minus average activity for plain words.
Sham vector A random direction of the same length as a concept vector.
Shared part The average of all concept vectors: what they have in common.
Specific part A concept vector minus the shared part: what is particular to that concept.
Lexical control A question that uses the detection question's words without asking about the model's state.
Offset How much further a question moved than control questions starting from the same point.
Injection strength α The multiplier applied to the vector before it is added. The dose.
ROC area Probability that a signal trial has a higher decision variable than a noise trial. 0.5 = chance.
d′ Sensitivity index, here √2 × z(ROC area).
Naive contrast Detection shift minus control shift.
Gap Concept-versus-sham ROC area on the detection question minus its average on control questions.
Bootstrap interval 95% range of a mean when the concepts are resampled 2,000 times.
Valid condition At least half of the model's probability is on “Yes” or “No”.

Every number above is read from this run's trial tables.
