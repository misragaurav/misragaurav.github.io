Same words, different meaning: re-testing emotion probes with lexically matched stimuli

Project 4, round 2. Gemma 3 4B, 32 items x 8 versions, all versions of an item built from exactly the same words. Headline numbers at layer 22, fixed before any data. Round 1's report: ../p4_gemma-3-4b-it/report.html. Design: P4M_PRD.md.

Version 3, 18 November 2026. Version 1 used the pre-registered model rater; version 2 added a second, stronger model rater (Qwen3-8B); this version adds my own ratings of the salience-control swap (section 4, last part, and summary point 5). Everything else is unchanged. Version 2: report_v2.html.

The short version

Round 1 had a flaw I couldn't argue my way around: a classifier that only counts words, never looking inside the model, could name the emotion of a passage 92% of the time. So I rebuilt the stimulus set so that words alone can't do anything: 32 short scenes, each in 8 versions made of exactly the same words, where swapping two phrases flips whether the outcome is good or bad, whether it's happening now or remembered, and whether it happens to someone who matters or to a crash-test dummy. Then I took the instruments I built in round 1, froze them, and pointed them at the new passages.

  1. The instruments survive the lexical control. Built only on round-1 passages, they still rank the good-outcome version of a scene above its word-identical bad-outcome twin 91% to 92% of the time at layer 22, and the now-version above the remembered one 94% to 98% of the time. A word-count classifier scores exactly 50% by construction, and a word-pair (bigram) classifier built on round 1 scores 45% and 41%.
  2. Meaning shows up with depth. The cleanest test uses only the closing sentences, which are identical in every version, word for word and in the same order. There, every instrument starts near chance in the first layers and climbs to 86% to 90% for valence from the middle of the model on. Same stimulus, different context, different representation, and you can watch it being composed.
  3. Salience control is the weak spot, and it's the one that matters for specificity. Telling "this happened to her husband" from "this happened to a training mannequin" (same words) is harder: 77% to 86% on the whole passage, and only 55% to 72% on the identical tail.
  4. The distress-specificity picture changed. With identical words, the emotion-vector distress score (A) barely separates a real disaster from the same disaster happening to a dummy (59% of scenes). The emotion-word score (B), the round-1 laggard, does it best (88%). In round 1 those two were ranked the other way round. Every distress score does show a genuine valence-by-time interaction on the identical tail (about +0.11 SD, interval clear of zero): it rises for bad and now more than the main effects would predict, which is what a distress-specific reading should do.

  5. My pre-registered manipulation check failed with the rater I planned, so I added a stronger one, and then rated the hardest swap myself. The planned rater (Qwen2.5-7B) moved its valence ratings with the outcome swap by about 1.6 points, short of my 2-point rule, and not at all with the salience-control swap: every scene was excluded. A stronger rater (Qwen3-8B), run with the same rules, gets the outcome and time swaps (+2.4 and +1.0 points) but still barely moves with salience control (+0.3), so 3 scenes pass. So I rated the salience-control swap myself on the 23 scenes it failed: to me the dummy version was +2.1 points more pleasant on average, and every one of the 23 scenes passed the 1-point rule. The swap is in the text; the model raters weren't registering it. With my ratings deciding that rule, 8 scenes pass all three pre-registered rules, and on those the round-1 instruments rank good above bad and now above remembered in every scene. The main analysis still uses all 32 scenes; wherever a reader (model or me) registered a swap, the instruments did as well or better (sections 4 and 5).

  6. Stimulation: a dose-response, mostly but not entirely explained by the sham. Adding the round-1 distress direction to Gemma's activity while it answers neutral questions makes its answers rated less pleasant, in a graded way (-0.3, -0.9, -1.3, -2.0 points on a 9-point scale at doses 0.05 to 0.2 with the planned rater; -0.2, -1.5, -2.8, -3.5 with the stronger one). A random direction of the same size does almost as much once it starts breaking the text (11 of 12 answers collapse at the top dose). In the window before anything collapses, at dose 0.10, the stronger rater scores distress-steered answers -1.0 points below sham-steered ones (95% interval -1.9 to -0.1); the planned rater points the same way (-0.8) without reaching significance. The direction built from word-identical pairs turned out to encode the salience-control swap (who the event happens to), not distress: its "distress" and "elation" versions are 98% the same direction, both stay fluent at every dose, and the "distress" version is consistently a little more negative than the "elation" one.

1. What was wrong with round 1

In round 1 I rebuilt two published emotion instruments on Gemma 3 4B and tested them on 384 passages I'd had written for the job: eight emotions, a neutral baseline, and three "salience controls" (urgent, loud, surprising but unemotional). Instrument A is the emotion-vector recipe from Sofroniew et al. (2026); instrument B is the logit-lens emotion-word score from Soligo et al. (2026); C is a plain logistic-regression probe, the MVPA decoder of the piece.

The headline number from round 1 that bothered me most wasn't any of the instrument numbers. It was this: a bag-of-words classifier that never looks inside the model named the emotion of a held-out passage 92% of the time. I had banned every emotion word, and it still worked, because each emotion has its own way of talking. Desperate passages plead, excited ones exclaim, calm ones say "slowly". So when the probe hit 100% and the emotion vectors hit 86%, I couldn't say whether they were reading Gemma's representation of the emotion or just the vocabulary that comes with it. The cross-perspective transfer results (build on stories, test on the assistant's own voice) were the only part of round 1 that clearly went beyond wording.

Anyone who has built a decoder of a subjective state knows this worry. In my 2017 Journal of Neurophysiology paper we classified high versus low pain perception from 128-channel EEG with about 90% leave-one-out accuracy, using theta and gamma power over medial prefrontal cortex and low-beta power over contralateral sensorimotor cortex. The first question any pain decoder has to answer is whether it reads pain or something that travels with pain: stimulus intensity, salience, arousal (Iannetti and Mouraux made that case forcefully for the "pain matrix" in 2010). Round 1's 92% is the language-model version of that problem: the "stimulus" (the words) co-varied with the "percept" (the emotion), so a decoder could succeed without the percept at all.

2. The fix: hold the words fixed, flip the meaning

The cleanest way I know to separate a stimulus property from a percept is to hold the stimulus constant and change the percept some other way. That was the logic of my pain-modulation work at Florida. In the 2014 PLoS ONE dose-response study the thermode temperature was calibrated per person and then fixed; what changed was how hard the other hand squeezed. If pain ratings moved, the heat couldn't be the reason. Here the "stimulus" is the set of words, so round 2 holds the words fixed and lets the meaning change.

Each of 32 short scenes has three swap slots. A slot is a sentence like "The scan showed {0}, not {1}." with two fillers, here "a bruise" and "a broken neck". One version reads "a bruise, not a broken neck"; the other reads "a broken neck, not a bruise". Same words, opposite meaning. Three binary slots per scene give 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 versions, all containing exactly the same words:

Slot What it flips Example (football injury scene)
Salience control Does the event happen to someone who matters, or to a dummy, a drill, a stranger on TV? This is round 1's salience control, rebuilt with identical words "The player lying still on the grass was Lucia's husband, not a mannequin used in the medics' training session" (and the reverse)
Valence Good or bad outcome "The scan showed a bruise, not a broken neck" (and the reverse)
Time Happening now, or remembered from long ago "This was happening this minute, not something from a season long ago" (and the reverse)

Every scene ends with one or two tail sentences that are word-for-word identical in all eight versions ("A stretcher was carried on at a run. The referee's whistle blew three times, sharp and high."). Gemma reads left to right, so those tail tokens are physically identical across versions and differ only in what came before them. It's the language-model version of presenting the same probe stimulus after two different cues.

Two checks that the design does what it claims. The build script confirms that all 8 versions of every scene contain the same multiset of words, and the recording script confirms they contain the same multiset of tokens (the units Gemma actually reads): no item had any difference. And at layer 0, the activity averaged over a passage is just the average of the token embeddings, so it must be identical across the 8 versions. It is: the spread within an item is 1.4e-08, against 0.083 between items. That's my equivalent of checking the thermode actually delivered the same temperature on every trial before believing anything about the ratings.

3. What I did with it

Recording. Same as round 1: one forward pass per passage on Gemma 3 4B in 32-bit, residual stream averaged over the passage's tokens at every one of the 34 layers (first 10 tokens skipped). I saved two averages per passage: over the whole passage ("full") and over the identical tail only ("tail").

The instruments were built on round 1 and tested on round 2. I did not rebuild anything on the new passages. A, B, C and C+ (the probe trained with the round-1 salience controls as counter-examples) were built exactly as in round 1, from the 341 round-1 passages that passed that round's manipulation check. Then they were applied, frozen, to the 256 new passages. That's the test a pain signature faces when someone applies it to a new cohort with new stimuli. I added:

  • W, the round-1 word-count classifier, applied to the new passages. All 8 versions of a scene have the same words, so W gives them identical scores. It's the chance level by construction.
  • C-new, a probe trained on the new passages themselves (leave one topic out), asking how much of each factor is linearly readable at each layer. A word-count classifier trained the same way scores 50% on every factor, by construction.

The statistic is a paired 2AFC. Because every version has seven word-identical siblings, I compare versions within a scene: does the instrument give the good-outcome version a higher valence reading than the bad-outcome version with the same words? The proportion of scene pairs ranked correctly is a two-alternative forced-choice accuracy (chance 50%; it's the paired equivalent of an ROC area). Intervals are bootstrap 95% intervals over scenes. I also report the paired difference in standard-deviation units, which is just Cohen's d for repeated measures, for the effect size.

For valence and arousal I used each instrument's eight emotion scores: valence = mean of the four positive emotions minus mean of the four negative ones; arousal = mean of the five high-arousal emotions minus the three low-arousal ones. For salience control I used each instrument's own "emotional versus neutral" reading. For specificity I used each instrument's round-1 distress score, unchanged.

4. Did the passages carry what they were meant to? My pre-registered check failed, and the rater turned out to be a weak judge

Same blind rater as round 1 (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, a different model from Gemma, never told the labels): for each passage, how pleasant is the main character's feeling (1 to 9), how intense is the text (1 to 9). Because the design is matched, I set the exclusion rules per scene, in advance: a scene stays only if (1) its good versions are rated at least 2 points more pleasant than its bad versions, (2) the salience-control swap makes the bad-and-now version at least 1 point more pleasant, and (3) the now version is at least 0.5 points more intense than the remembered one.

Result: 0 of 32 scenes passed. 10 passed the valence rule, 19 the arousal rule, 0 the salience-control rule. Averaged over scenes, the rater's valence rose by 1.6 points from bad to good outcomes, its arousal rose by 0.8 points from remembered to now, and the salience-control swap moved valence by -0.03 points, which is nothing.

How to read it: left, the rater's mean ratings in each of the six cells; right, how often it answered three plain comprehension questions correctly (explained next).

So I had a choice: throw the data away, or work out whether the stimuli or the check had failed. The rater was asked how the scene feels. A scene with a tackle that cracks across the pitch, a stretcher, a broken neck... feels bad, whether the neck belongs to Lucia's husband or to a training mannequin. That's a defensible reading of the question, and it doesn't tell me whether the rater understood the swap. So I added a check I should have planned in the first place: three plain comprehension questions per passage, no feelings involved. Does the event happen to a real person someone cares about, or to a dummy/drill/recording? Did things turn out well or badly? Is it happening now or remembered? Options in both orders, averaged.

Comprehension result. Probability of the correct answer, averaged over all 256 passages: salience control 70%, outcome 72%, time 90% (chance 50%). The rater understands the time swap well. It understands the salience-control swap only partly, and on the outcome it sits at exactly 50% on 10 of 32 scenes, which is what you get when a model picks whichever option is listed first regardless of the text; on most of the other scenes it's right. So the affect ratings weren't failing for some subtle reason: a 7-billion-parameter model reading these sentences often doesn't work out who the event happened to, or whether it turned out well. That cuts both ways. The failed manipulation check doesn't show the stimuli are bad, and this rater can't show they're good either. That needs human readers, and it's the first thing I'd fix.

I'm therefore treating this as a deviation from my plan and saying so plainly: the main analysis uses all 32 scenes. My planned sensitivity check, re-running everything on the scenes whose three swaps the rater understood (probability correct at least 0.75 on all three questions, a rule I fixed before looking at the numbers), turned out to be empty in practice: only 1 scene passed all three. So I did the next best thing, post hoc and labelled as such: for each factor separately, I kept the scenes whose swap for that factor the rater understood (14 scenes for outcome, 10 for salience control, 25 for time) and re-ran that factor's test. Those results are at the end of section 5.

This is the same distinction I'd draw in a pain study between a manipulation check and a comprehension check. If participants don't rate the "placebo" cream as more effective, you first check that they heard the instructions, before concluding the expectation manipulation failed.

A second, stronger rater

A 7-billion-parameter rater that answers "outcome" questions at chance on a third of the scenes isn't a fair judge, so I downloaded a stronger and newer one, Qwen3-8B (a different family from Gemma; its "thinking" mode switched off so I can read the answer probabilities the same way). I logged the change in the design document before it rated anything, and applied exactly the same pre-registered rules.

  • Affect ratings. Averaged over scenes, valence rose by 2.4 points from bad to good outcomes (rule: 2.0), arousal by 1.0 from remembered to now (rule: 0.5), and the salience-control swap moved valence by +0.3 (rule: 1.0). 17 scenes passed the valence rule, 14 the arousal rule, 9 the salience-control rule, and 3 passed all three. The two raters' ratings of the 256 passages correlate 0.74 for valence and 0.72 for arousal.
  • Comprehension. Salience control 72%, outcome 81%, time 92% correct (5 scenes pass all three at 0.75).
  • The analysis I pre-registered, finally run. On the 3 scenes that pass the pre-registered rules with this rater, the round-1 instruments rank good above bad in 100% / 100% / 100% of pairs (A / B / C) and now above remembered in 100% / 100% / 100%; salience control 67% / 83% / 67%; distress against the same scene with a dummy 67% / 67% / 67%. With 3 scenes these are anecdotes with percentages attached, but they're the numbers my plan asked for, and they point the same way as everything else.

So the stronger rater largely rescues the outcome and time manipulations and confirms they're legible to a reader, while salience control stays hard for both raters. By both model raters' accounts, a mannequin's broken neck feels almost as bad as a husband's, even though they can report who was involved when asked directly (72% correct). Was that a property of the stimuli or of the raters? Only a human reader could say, so I read them myself.

My own ratings of the salience-control swap (new in this version)

What I did. I rated the two passages the salience-control rule compares (the bad event happening now, once to the real person and once to the dummy) for each of the 23 scenes where Qwen3-8B failed that rule: 46 passages. I used the same question and the same 1-to-9 scale the model raters got ("How pleasant or unpleasant is the feeling of the speaker or main character in this text?"). The passages came in a shuffled order, with the two versions of a scene never next to each other, and the labels and model ratings were out of view. That's as close to the model raters' conditions as I could get, but it isn't blind: I wrote the design, so I knew which version I was reading as soon as I read it.

Result. I rated the real-person versions 1.7 on average and the dummy versions 3.8: a gap of +2.1 points. All 23 of 23 scenes passed the 1-point rule (the smallest gap was 1 point, in 6 scenes). On the same passages, Qwen3-8B rated the real-person versions 3.4 and the dummy versions 3.1, and Qwen2.5-7B 3.9 and 3.8: no gap at all, if anything the wrong way round. My ratings barely correlate with either model's on these passages (r = 0.13 and 0.11), while the two models correlate 0.71 with each other. That correlation is on a selected set (the scenes Qwen3-8B got wrong), so it overstates the disagreement in general, but the direction is clear: the two model raters share a way of reading these scenes that a person doesn't.

How to read it: left, one row per scene. Filled dots are the real-person version, open dots the dummy version; a line joins a rater's two ratings of the same scene. My dots (black) always have the open dot to the right, i.e. the dummy version is more pleasant. The models' two dots for a scene are close together, often in the wrong order. Right, the gap per scene; the dashed line is the 1-point rule.

So, as far as one reader who isn't blind can tell, the salience-control swap is in the text. A reader who knows that a mannequin isn't a husband rates the scenes very differently; the model raters, as raters, don't. In pain-study terms, the stimulus was fine and the rating instrument was insensitive, which is a different failure from a stimulus that doesn't work, and it means the salience-control tests in sections 5 and 6 are tests of a manipulation that does what it says.

What my notes turned up. I left notes on 6 passages, and they're worth more than the numbers. In three scenes it isn't clear whether the main character knows it's not her son, or that it was only a demo page, at the moment the passage ends; I rated them assuming she knows. One has an internal contradiction (a plumbing test rig with soaked photographs), one leaves it unclear whose client it is, and one read as artificial to me. The question asks about the character's feeling, so a character who doesn't yet know it's the dummy should feel bad, and a rater that reads it that way isn't wrong. That's probably part of why the model raters failed, and those scenes need rewriting before anyone else rates them.

Three things stand out, and one caveat. Valence and arousal are perfect on the whole passage, as they were on Qwen3-8B's 3 scenes. The salience-control swap is about as readable on these scenes as on all 32, and the ranking from section 6 holds: on the distress-versus-dummy test B has the largest effect (+0.21 SD, 95% interval +0.05 to +0.39), and A the smallest (+0.08 SD, -0.01 to +0.17). And the distress-like valence-by-time interaction on the identical tail is about twice as large on these scenes as on all 32 (A +0.22 SD against +0.11; B +0.24 against +0.12; C +0.20 against +0.11; all intervals clear of zero), while the random-word sham shows none (-0.01). The cleaner the manipulation, the stronger the signature, which is what you'd want to see if the signature is real. One thing doesn't improve: on the identical tail alone, the salience-control swap isn't readable on these scenes (38% / 69% / 44%), and the distress-versus-dummy test is at chance for B and C. Salience control is carried by the slot sentences themselves more than by how the shared ending is read, here as on all 32 scenes. With 8 scenes the percentages move in steps of about 6 points (a tie counts as half) and the intervals are wide, so I read all of this as consistent with the main analysis, not as a separate result.

5. Result 1: can the round-1 instruments tell word-identical versions apart?

This is round 1's section 6 redone properly. In round 1 I asked "which of eight emotions is this?" and the answer was contaminated by vocabulary. Here the question is narrower and clean: given two versions of the same scene with the same words, does the instrument move in the right direction?

So the round-1 instruments were not only reading vocabulary. Frozen, trained on different passages with different words, they rank matched versions correctly most of the time. The effect sizes are modest (+0.41 SD to +0.54 SD for valence): the swap moves the readout consistently, not hugely. That's what you'd expect when every token is shared and only a few phrases change places.

The word-order loophole, and how I closed it. Identical words aren't identical word order. "This was happening tonight, not a night from thirty years ago" and its swap contain different two-word sequences at the slot boundaries ("was happening" against "was a"). A word-pair classifier built on round 1 doesn't benefit (41% for arousal; it's below chance), but word pairs learned within round 2 decode the time swap perfectly (100%). That fits a worrying detail: the round-1 arousal readouts are already right 97% (A) and 94% (C) of the time after the very first block, which smells like local syntax ("was X" versus "not X") rather than anything deep.

The tail closes the loophole. The closing sentences are the same tokens in the same order in all eight versions, so nothing about the words, their pairs or their positions can differ. The only way their averaged activity can differ is through what the model carried forward from earlier in the passage.

Same layout, but the activity is averaged over the identical closing sentences only.

On the tail, every instrument starts near chance in the first five layers (valence: A 50%, B 60%, C 60%) and climbs to 86%, 87% and 90% on average over layers 16 to 33. Arousal does the same (66% rising to 86% for the probe). Salience control barely gets going on the tail (72% (58% to 84%) for B, the best of the three, at layer 22).

That's the cleanest evidence in either round that these instruments read meaning: physically identical tokens, read through different context, end up on different sides of an emotion axis that was learned from completely different text, and the separation appears only at the depths where the model composes meaning, not at the input. In neuroscience terms it's the difference between an early sensory area, which responds the same way to the same stimulus whatever it means, and a later area whose response depends on the context the stimulus arrives in.

The instruments do as well or better on the scenes the raters understood: with the planned rater, every one of the 14 outcome-understood scenes is ranked correctly by all three instruments on the whole passage, and the stronger rater's subsets give the same picture, including B's lead on the real-versus-dummy distress test. The scenes that are hard for the raters are the ones that are hard for Gemma's readouts. That's what you'd expect if both are reading the same meaning, and the opposite of what you'd expect if the instruments were picking up some artefact a reader can't see. The subsets are small, so the intervals are wide (up to plus or minus 30 points for the salience-control subsets).

6. Result 2: is a distress score specific when the words are identical?

This is round 1's section 8 redone. In round 1 the hard comparison was distress against the salience controls: intense passages (urgent logistics, crashes, surprises) with no feeling in them. But those controls used different words from the distress passages, so a good separation could have been lexical. Now the salience control is the distress passage with two phrases swapped: same crash, same broken neck, but it's the mannequin's neck.

On the identical tail, every distress score has a clear interaction from about layer 16 up (layer 22: A +0.11 SD, B +0.12, C +0.11, C+ +0.11; all intervals clear of zero), and the word-based scores have none. Including B, which in round 1 showed the wrong interaction: that was probably a lexical artefact too.

7. Result 3: a dose-response curve for steering

Why a dose-response curve. In round 1 I steered at two doses and got an ambiguous answer. Two doses isn't a dose-response curve. In our 2014 PLoS ONE paper the whole point was the dose-response: participants squeezed at 5%, 25% or 50% of their maximum voluntary contraction while a thermode heated the other hand, and analgesia grew with force (0.10, 0.32 and 0.62 points on a 0 to 10 scale). Only the 50% condition was significant, and only with the hot stimulus (about 6/10); at the merely warm temperature, nothing happened at any force. Two lessons from that paper shaped this experiment: you need enough dose to see anything, and you need something to modulate.

I'm also certified to deliver tDCS, and the first thing you learn there is that the dose-response isn't a straight line. Dose is current times time times electrode montage, and more isn't simply more: Batsikadze and colleagues (2013) found that raising cathodal stimulation of motor cortex from 1 mA to 2 mA flipped its after-effect from inhibition to excitation. So I didn't assume the steering effect would grow monotonically, and I tracked the side effect (the answers falling apart) alongside the intended effect, the way you'd track skin tingling or discomfort alongside the target outcome.

The design. Ratings in the table below are from the planned rater; section 4 explains the second, stronger one, and both appear in the contrasts further down. Four directions, each scaled to the same length, added at layer 22 at every token while Gemma answers the same twelve neutral prompts as in round 1 ("Describe a bookshelf in an office", "How are you feeling right now?" and so on), at four doses: 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2 of the typical size of the activity at that layer. The blind rater scores every answer, and a simple detector counts answers that collapse into repetition ("panic panic panic", "There There There").

  • Round-1 distress: instrument A's distress direction, the one I steered with in round 1.
  • Matched distress: the mean activity of the real-person bad-outcome versions minus the dummy bad-outcome versions. Same words on both sides, so this direction can't carry vocabulary.
  • Matched elation: the same contrast for good outcomes.
  • Random: the sham, same length, no meaning.

The first surprise came before any steering. The matched distress and matched elation directions have a cosine of 0.98: they're essentially the same direction. Both are "real person minus dummy", and that difference is almost the same whether the outcome was good or bad. With the words held fixed, what the contrast isolates is the salience-control swap: someone who matters is involved. It isn't distress. Both are nearly orthogonal to the round-1 distress direction (+0.03) and to the random one.

What I read from it.

  • The round-1 distress direction has a clean, graded dose-response (planned rater; the stronger rater's numbers are steeper): -0.31, -0.92, -1.33, -2.03 points of valence as the dose goes from 0.05 to 0.2, monotonic, like the analgesia in the 2014 paper growing with force. But the sham catches up. The random direction barely does anything at the low doses and then wrecks the text (0, 0, 4, 11 of 12 answers collapsed), and the rater scores wrecked text as unpleasant. Against the sham at the same dose, with the planned rater the round-1 distress direction is never reliably more negative. With the stronger rater it is, at dose 0.10, the highest dose before any answer collapses, and at no other dose (table below). That's one significant cell out of eight, in the place it should be, with the other rater pointing the same way; I read it as a real but narrow effect.
  • The matched (salience-control) directions are tolerated at every dose: 0, 0, 0, 0 and 0, 0, 0, 2 collapsed answers out of 12 at the four doses, against 0, 0, 4, 11 for the sham. Directions the model actually uses in reading can be pushed much harder than arbitrary ones before anything breaks. That's the therapeutic-window idea: the usable range of a dose is set by where the side effects start, and that depends on what you stimulate, not only how hard.
  • The small difference between the two matched directions behaves like a weak dose-response. Matched distress minus matched elation, on the same prompts: -0.27 (-0.60 to -0.02) at 0.05; -0.46 (-1.36 to +0.47) at 0.1; -0.63 (-1.40 to +0.04) at 0.15; -0.98 (-2.22 to +0.20) at 0.2. With the stronger rater: -0.02 (-0.18 to +0.15) at 0.05; -0.81 (-1.61 to +0.01) at 0.1; -0.73 (-1.34 to -0.11) at 0.15; -0.67 (-1.62 to +0.20) at 0.2. Never in the wrong direction, with one dose's interval clear of zero for each rater. Twelve prompts isn't enough to call it more than a consistent tilt: the 2% of the matched directions that differs between bad and good outcomes nudges the answers the expected way.
  • Arousal barely moves for any direction within the fluent range.

The tDCS lesson applies. Two things go together in tDCS: an active session means little without a sham at the same current and duration, and the dose-response can be flat, graded or even reversed. Both showed up here. Without the same-length random direction, the round-1 distress direction would have looked like a clean dose-dependent "distress induction". With it, most of that slope is what any big enough push does to a language model, and what's left is a narrow window around dose 0.10 where the distress direction does something the sham doesn't. That's a familiar shape from stimulation work: a usable range bounded below by "too weak to matter" and above by "side effects swamp everything".

A measurement-level caveat, from my own work. In our 2017 Cerebral Cortex paper, ongoing pain shortened reaction times through premotor beta suppression without changing movement kinematics, accuracy or EMG at the painful site. If we'd only measured the movement's form, we'd have concluded pain did nothing. The matched directions here left the answers fluent and changed their rated tone only slightly; whether they changed Gemma's internal state, measured with the same instruments during the answer, is a separate question I haven't asked yet.

9. Limits, honestly

  • One small model (Gemma 3 4B). The papers used Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Gemma 27B.
  • 32 scenes. The intervals resample scenes, so they're wide-ish, and with 8 topics I'm not pretending they're more than approximate.
  • One author, one formula. All scenes were written by Claude to one template, and every slot sentence has the same "X, not Y" shape. Language models are known to be shaky with negation, and the rater often was (section 4), which may be part of why its checks failed. Gemma's readouts mostly weren't, but a second author and swaps that don't rely on "not" would make the set more robust.
  • Order still differs. The words are identical, but their order isn't, so a model that only tracked bigrams could in principle find a difference at the slot boundaries. The tail analysis guards against this: the tail tokens are identical and in identical order.
  • Salience control isn't a pure "no emotion" control. A mannequin's broken neck is less upsetting than a husband's, but a scene with sirens and a stretcher is still a scene with sirens and a stretcher. That's the point of the control, but it also means "salience control" here is "same events, much less at stake", not "zero affect".
  • The human rater is me. I rated only the salience-control swap, only on the 23 scenes the stronger model rater failed, and I wrote the design, so I knew what each passage was meant to be as soon as I read it. My ratings show the swap is legible to a person; they aren't a blind manipulation check. Outcome and time are still certified only by a model rater.
  • Reading, not feeling. Gemma reads these scenes; nothing here puts Gemma itself in a distressing situation. That's project 5.

10. What I'd do next

  1. Take the right control into project 5 (recovery from distress). Project 5 is the language-model version of my 2013 Journal of Pain work on non-drug modulation of pain with force and working-memory tasks: induce a distress-like state in a conversation, try distraction or a cognitive load, and see whether the internal reading recovers or only the report does. What this project adds is the control: a distress reading has to be checked against the same scene with nobody at stake, and instrument A alone fails that check. B and C+ held up better, so I'd carry all three and report them side by side.
  2. Blind human ratings of all 256 matched passages by several readers who don't know the design, as a proper manipulation check, and a rewrite of the scenes my own ratings flagged (mainly: make it explicit that the main character knows it's the dummy).
  3. Token-resolved readouts. Averaging over a passage is like averaging an EEG epoch. A time-resolved view (which token does the distress reading jump on?) is the obvious next step, and it's the same question as predicting anger onset from wearable-EEG wavelet features, which I did as a side project: onset is about timing, and an average throws timing away.
  4. Bigger models, to see whether salience control becomes as readable as valence with scale.
