Do emotion readouts in a language model agree, and are they specific? 18 models

Gaurav Misra. 18 open-weights models, 0.27 to 7.3 billion weights; the Gemma study's stimuli, instruments and analysis code, unchanged, applied to every model. All on one laptop, with no training.

Short version. The Gemma study (full report) tested two published ways of reading emotion from a language model's activations on one model, Gemma 3 4B. Here the same tests run on 18 models.

  • Every instrument decodes emotion in every model where it is defined, but so does counting words: on round 1's passages a word-count classifier reaches 92%. Median accuracy: A 86%, B 58%, C 100%.
  • The two published instruments agree only moderately. Median correlation between A and B for the same emotion: 0.39 (range 0.09 to 0.62).
  • Almost no distress reading is specific. Intense but unemotional passages produce a median 47% (A), 46% (B) and 43% (C) of the response to real distress. The exceptions (an instrument at or below 10%): OLMo-2 1B (B −17%). A share below zero means salience moved that reading the opposite way to distress.
  • With identical words, the meaning is still readable. On round 2's word-matched scenes, the probe built on round 1 picks the right version from the identical closing sentences above chance for valence in 18 of 18 models (a word-counting classifier is at exactly 50% by construction).
  • The hardest specificity test mostly fails. On round 2's scenes with identical words, telling a distress scene from the same scene happening to a mannequin: median B 56%, A 47%, C 42% (chance 50%). Gemma's B (88%) was the exception, not the rule.
  • Steering: adding instrument A's distress direction (from round 1) makes a model's answers rated less pleasant than adding a random direction of the same size, but only slightly: at the top dose by 0.43 points on a 9-point scale, averaged over models (95% interval 0.31 to 0.56). Averaged over doses it points that way in 18 of 18 models; for a single model its 95% interval clears zero at one dose or more in 7, and never the other way. The direction built from word-identical scenes behaves the same whether it was built from a bad or a good outcome, in every model, as in Gemma.

1. The question

Two 2026 papers read emotion from a language model's internal activations, each with its own instrument: A, emotion vectors (a direction per emotion, the average activation over stories about that emotion minus the average over all emotions), and B, emotion-word scores (the activation read through the model's own vocabulary, the "logit lens", and scored on lists of emotion words). The Gemma study rebuilt both, plus a trained linear probe C as a yardstick, and tested them the way a brain signature of pain is tested: do two instruments that claim to measure the same thing agree (convergent validity), and does a "distress" reading respond to distress or to anything intense (specificity, the pain-versus-salience problem)? It also rebuilt the stimuli with identical words (round 2), so that wording could not do the instruments' work.

The Gemma study answered these questions for Gemma 3 4B. This project asks whether the answers hold across 18 models from 7 families.

2. What was done

Nothing new was designed. The Gemma study's code was imported and pointed at each model:

  1. Record. Each model reads the 384 round-1 passages (eight emotions, neutral, and three kinds of intense but unemotional salience control: urgent, loud, surprising; each in four perspectives) and the 256 round-2 passages (32 scenes, eight versions each with identical words). For every passage, the activation at every layer is averaged over its tokens.
  2. Build and test the instruments. A, B and C are built from seven topics and tested on the eighth, for each topic in turn, so that no instrument is scored on passages whose topic it has seen. The 43 passages that failed the Gemma study's manipulation check are excluded here too (that check rated the text, not the model).
  3. Round 2. The round-1 instruments, frozen, score the word-matched scenes.
  4. Steer. Four directions (matched distress, matched elation, round-1 distress, and a random direction of the same length) are added at four doses while the model answers 12 neutral prompts.

One setting changes per model: the layer. The Gemma study fixed its headline layer in advance at two thirds of the way through the model (layer 22 of Gemma's 34). The same rule gives each model's reference layer. Models up to 4 billion weights ran in 32-bit numbers; the two 7B models in float16 and Gemma 4 E2B in bfloat16.

Check: does the code reproduce the Gemma study? Gemma 3 4B was run again through this project's code. Its numbers beside the Gemma study's:

3. Results

Decoding: can the instruments tell the emotions apart?

Figure 1 shows, for each model, how often each instrument names the right one of eight emotions for a passage from a topic it never saw. Chance is 12.5%. The dotted line is the catch that the Gemma study found: a classifier that only counts words, with no access to the model, reaches 92% on these passages, because each emotion still has its own vocabulary even with emotion words banned. High decoding here therefore says little about whether the model represents emotion; round 2 is the stronger test.

Agreement: do two instruments measure the same thing?

Figure 2 shows the convergent correlation: across passages, how closely two instruments' readings of the same emotion move together. In a measurement science this is the first validity check: two thermometers in the same room should agree. Across models the two published instruments (A with B) correlate 0.09 to 0.62. With model size: Spearman ρ = 0.31 (p = 0.22).

Specificity: distress, or anything intense?

Figure 3 is the Gemma study's specificity measure. For each instrument's distress reading, it compares two effects, each against neutral passages: real distress, and the intense but unemotional salience controls. The bar is the second as a share of the first. A specific distress reading would be near 0%: salience would leave it untouched. In the Gemma study it was 45% (A), 50% (B) and 43% (C). Across the 18 models, A ranges 39% to 62%, B −17% to 56%, C 39% to 48%. With model size (instrument A): Spearman ρ = −0.08 (p = 0.76).

Round 2: meaning with the words held fixed

In round 2 every scene comes in eight versions that contain exactly the same words, and differ only in how three phrases are arranged: whether the outcome is good or bad (valence), whether it is happening now or remembered (arousal), and whether it happens to someone who matters or to a dummy (stake). The closing sentences are word for word identical in all eight, so activity averaged over them can differ only because of what came before. Figure 4 asks whether the round-1 probe, never trained on these scenes, can tell two versions apart from those identical sentences alone.

Figure 5 is round 2's hardest specificity test: the distress version of a scene against the same scene with the same bad outcome happening to a mannequin or a drill. The words are identical; only whether anyone who matters is involved changes. In the Gemma study, B separated them best (88%) and A worst (59%).

Across models the medians are B 56%, A 47%, C 42%: B separates distress from the dummy version best, as in the Gemma study, but far less well than there (Gemma: B 88%, C 72%, A 59%), and the instruments' order otherwise changes from model to model. Each instrument is below chance, ranking the dummy version higher more often than the real one, in many models: A in 10 of 18, B in 5 of 17, C in 12 of 18. For A and C, that is most of the models: their distress readings on these word-matched scenes do not track whether anyone who matters is hurt. B's does, modestly, in most models.

Weak cases. Instrument B barely decodes emotion in Gemma 4 E2B (18%; chance is 12.5%), possibly because of the soft cap on its scores (section 5). A and B hardly agree at all in Gemma 1 2B (0.10), Gemma 1.1 2B (0.09).

4. Steering: does adding a distress direction change what the model says?

For each model, four directions were added to its activity at the reference layer while it answered 12 neutral prompts ("Describe a bookshelf in an office", "How are you feeling right now?"), at four doses (5%, 10%, 15% and 20% of the typical size of the activity there), plus the 12 unsteered answers: 3,672 answers from 18 models, written with no randomness. The directions:

  • Round-1 distress: instrument A's distress direction, built from round 1's passages.
  • Matched distress and matched elation: built from round 2's word-identical scenes (a scene happening to someone who matters, minus the same words with a dummy), for a bad and for a good outcome.
  • Random: a random direction of the same length, the sham.

A push of any kind can make an answer odder or more broken, and broken text can read as unpleasant. So the question is not whether a distress direction makes the answers less pleasant, but whether it does so more than the random direction of the same size, compared prompt by prompt. That is the test the Gemma study ran.

Rating. GPT Astra rated every answer for valence (how pleasant, 1 to 9) and arousal (how intense, 1 to 9), one workbook per model. The workbooks held only a number and the text, in shuffled order: nothing about the model, the direction, the dose or the prompt. The returned ratings were checked (every cell filled, whole numbers from 1 to 9, texts unchanged) before being joined back to the answers.

Figure 6 is the dose-response, averaged over models. The round-1 distress direction lowers the rated valence step by step as the dose rises (−0.16, −0.30, −0.48, −0.55 points at the four doses), while the random direction barely moves it (−0.03, −0.10, −0.13, −0.12). The two matched directions, if anything, raise it slightly.

The paired test. Figure 7 shows, for every model and dose, the round-1 distress direction's rated valence minus the random direction's, prompt by prompt. Averaged over models, the difference is 5%: −0.13 (95% interval −0.19 to −0.07); 10%: −0.19 (95% interval −0.31 to −0.10); 15%: −0.35 (95% interval −0.49 to −0.23); 20%: −0.43 (95% interval −0.56 to −0.31). It is below zero in 61 of the 72 model-and-dose cells, zero in 9 and above zero in 2. Averaged over doses it is below zero in 18 of 18 models and above zero in 0 (sign test p < 0.001). For a single model the interval clears zero at one or more doses in 7: Gemma 3 1B, Qwen2.5 1.5B, SmolLM2 1.7B, Qwen3 1.7B, Gemma 2 2B, Qwen2.5 3B, Gemma 3 4B; it never clears zero the other way. The largest difference is Gemma 3 1B at a dose of 20% (−1.17 points). Larger models do not show a larger effect (at the top dose, Spearman ρ = 0.30 with model size, p = 0.22).

Leaving out every pair in which either answer collapsed into repetition changes little (5%: −0.13; 10%: −0.20; 15%: −0.39; 20%: −0.49), so the effect is not just broken text being rated as unpleasant.

Check against the Gemma study. Gemma 3 4B's steered answers here are word for word the ones the Gemma study generated, so GPT Astra rated the same 204 texts that the Gemma study's two raters did. Its valence ratings agree moderately with theirs (r = 0.54 with its planned rater and r = 0.58 with its stronger rater). At a dose of 10%, the dose before anything collapses, it puts Gemma's distress-minus-random difference at −0.92 (95% interval −1.83 to −0.17). The Gemma study's stronger rater put the same difference at −1.02 (95% interval −1.93 to −0.06). So the one model the Gemma study tested gives the same answer with a third rater.

The matched directions. In the Gemma study, the "matched distress" and "matched elation" directions turned out to be nearly the same direction (cosine 0.98): with the words held fixed, what the contrast isolates is that someone who matters is involved, not whether the outcome is bad. The same holds in every model here (cosine 0.983 to 0.999). As that predicts, they steer the same way: matched distress minus matched elation is 0.00, −0.03, 0.00, −0.03 points at the four doses. Against the random direction both are, if anything, slightly more pleasant (matched distress minus random: 0.08, 0.17, 0.19, 0.20).

Breakdown. Figure 8 counts the answers that collapsed into repetition (one word making up more than a quarter of the answer, the same word four times in a row, or fewer than eight words). At the top dose, out of 12 answers per model, the random direction breaks an average of 1.6, the round-1 distress direction 0.2, and the matched directions 0.1 and 0.2. Gemma 3 4B, where the random direction broke 11 of 12, is the extreme: in most models a random push of this size leaves the text intact.

5. Limitations

  • One layer per model, chosen by a rule. Two thirds of the way through may not be each model's best layer for every instrument; the analysis saved every layer, so this can be checked.
  • Instrument B needs single-token emotion words. It scores each emotion by the words of that emotion that the model's vocabulary holds as one token. In Granite 3.3 2B (no single-token word for desperation) a category is empty, so B's distress score and everything built on it is not defined for that model, and B is left out of its numbers.
  • The stimuli were built for Gemma's project and are reused unchanged; so is the manipulation check, which rated the text.
  • Instrument B in Gemma 2 and Gemma 4 reads the vocabulary scores before those models' final soft cap at ±30. B standardises every word's score, so a constant scale would cancel, but the cap is not constant; for these two models B's readings are an approximation.
  • Number format. The 7B models and Gemma 4 E2B ran in 16-bit numbers, the rest in 32-bit.
  • Steering was rated by one rater, GPT Astra. It used a narrow part of the 1 to 9 scale (unsteered answers average 5.2 to 6.0 by model), which keeps every difference small in points. With 12 prompts per cell and whole-number ratings, a single model's interval is coarse; the pooled results across models are the firmer ones. On the Gemma answers that the Gemma study also rated, GPT Astra's valence ratings correlate 0.54 with the planned rater and 0.58 with the stronger rater.
  • One layer and four doses. Steering was done only at the reference layer, with one random direction per model.
  • Gemma 3n was left out: its layers carry four parallel copies of the activations instead of one residual stream.

6. What it means

Most of the Gemma study's conclusions about Gemma 3 4B hold across 18 models. The two published instruments agree only moderately, almost every distress reading also responds to intense but unemotional text, and much of round-1 decoding is available from word counts alone. At the same time, when the words are held fixed, whether a scene is pleasant or unpleasant can still be read from the identical closing sentences in 18 of 18 models, so the instruments are reading something beyond vocabulary. What does not generalise is Gemma's relatively good result on the hardest control: in most models, the distress readings of A and C cannot tell a scene in which someone who matters is hurt from the same words with a mannequin, and B does so only modestly. A reading from either instrument should not be called a measure of a specific emotion in any of these models without a control that holds the words fixed and removes what is at stake.

Steering adds a causal test, and its answer is a qualified yes. Adding instrument A's distress direction pushes the answers towards the unpleasant more than a random push of the same size does, step by step with dose, in all 18 models on average; but the push is small (about 0.4 of a point on a 9-point scale at the top dose, clear for a single model in only 7 of 18). A direction can change the model's output in the expected direction without being specific to distress when it is read: round 1 showed that the same direction also responds to intense but unemotional text. And the direction built with the words held fixed carries "someone who matters is involved", not distress, in every model.
