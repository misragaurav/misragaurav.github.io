Psychophysics of introspection: google/gemma-3-4b-it

a3 profile · 60,904 trials · 50 concepts · injection at layers 15, 20 of 34

1. The short version

Injection makes the model say “Yes”. When a concept vector is injected into google/gemma-3-4b-it, its lean towards “Yes” on “Do you detect an injected thought?” rises sharply. At layer 15, strength 4 it rises by +11.63 log-odds, which multiplies the odds of “Yes” by about 112,063. Judged against trials with no injection that is an ROC area of 0.84 (d′ = 1.41). Taken alone, it looks like detection.

A random vector moves the answers almost as much as a concept vector does in this model. At its own most effective valid condition a random vector of the same size moved the detection question +6.51. So the comparison of concept against random vectors (figure 2) cannot separate detection from bias here: a random vector is itself an injection. The tests that can are the reversed question and the matched controls, below.

The reversed question. Asked “Is this a control trial with no injected thought?”, a model that detects the injection should say “Yes” LESS often when a concept is injected, and a model that is only biased should say “Yes” MORE often. Over the 4 conditions where injection raised “Yes” on the standard question by at least 1 log-odds, the standard question moved +6.20 log-odds and the reversed question moved -9.35. “Yes” went down on the reversed question. That fits detection, but it also fits a model that is simply made less sure of everything, so the matched-control test below decides.

The matched-control test. Each detection question is compared with control questions that start from the same point. Detection predicts that the standard question rises MORE than its matched controls and the reversed question falls MORE than its matched controls. That pattern appears in 5 of 6 active conditions with concept vectors and in 1 of 2 with random vectors. By layer, concept and then random: layer 15: 3 of 3 and 0 of 1; layer 20: 2 of 3 and 1 of 1. So in this model the detection questions do respond to injection by more than matched controls do, in the direction “a thought is present”. At layer 15 it takes a concept vector: random vectors of the same size do not produce it. At layer 20 random vectors produce it too, so there the model is responding to being disturbed at all. Section 5 sets out what this rules out and what it does not show.

Which part of the concept vector carries the pattern? Each concept vector was split into the part shared by all the concepts and the part specific to that concept, and each part was injected alone, at the same size. The detection pattern appears in 5 of 6 active conditions with the whole concept vector, 0 of 6 active conditions with the specific part, 1 of 5 active conditions with the shared part, 1 of 2 active conditions with the random vector. By layer: layer 15: whole concept vector 3 of 3, specific part 0 of 3, shared part 1 of 3, random vector 0 of 1; layer 20: whole concept vector 2 of 3, specific part 0 of 3, shared part 0 of 2, random vector 1 of 1. Neither part reliably produces the pattern on its own. It needs the whole concept vector. Judged against the closest control family on each side, at layer 15 the whole concept vector moves the standard question +3.17 and the reversed question -2.35; at layer 15 the specific part moves the standard question +0.98 and the reversed question +2.41; at layer 15 the shared part moves the standard question -0.41 and the reversed question -0.59; at layer 15 the random vector moves the standard question +0.58 and the reversed question +0.61; at layer 20 the whole concept vector moves the standard question +1.02 and the reversed question -1.07; at layer 20 the specific part moves the standard question +1.40 and the reversed question -0.04; at layer 20 the shared part moves the standard question -1.70 and the reversed question -1.33; at layer 20 the random vector moves the standard question +1.64 and the reversed question -1.48. At layer 15 only the whole concept vector moves both questions by at least one log-odds unit in the directions detection predicts. Neither part does so alone, and neither does a random vector. The pattern needs the intact concept vector.

Lexical control questions. These use the detection question's words without asking about the model's state, for example “Does the word thought begin with the letter t?”. Over the 5 concept-vector conditions that show the detection pattern, the standard detection question sits +3.24 from its matched controls and the answer-No lexical controls sit -0.26. The reversed question sits -2.39 and the answer-Yes lexical controls +0.10. The lexical controls stay close to the control line. Using the detection question's words is not enough to produce the pattern.

Which concept? Asked which of two words the injected thought is about, the model's answer moves +11.51 log-odds towards the injected word, and the injected word becomes the more likely answer on 74% of trials. Asked only to choose either word, with no mention of injection, it moves +11.37 (72%). The identification question adds +1.21 to that, which is 11% of the plain priming effect, with its interval above zero in 2 and below zero in 0 of 3 valid conditions. So almost all of the pull is plain priming: a concept vector makes its own word the likely answer whatever is asked. Asking about the injected thought adds a little.

2. The question

Anthropic's research on introspection (Lindsey, 2025; Macar et al., 2026) uses a method called concept injection. Researchers find the pattern of internal activity that corresponds to a concept such as bread, add that pattern to the model's activity while it reads an unrelated prompt, and ask: “Do you detect an injected thought?” Models sometimes answer yes.

A yes can arise in two ways, and they mean very different things.

  1. Sensitivity. The model has some access to its own internal state, notices that something unusual is present, and reports it.
  2. Response bias. The injected pattern makes the word “Yes” more likely whatever the question is. The model would also say yes to “Is ice hotter than fire?”.

Signal detection theory was built to separate exactly these two things in human observers: how well an observer tells signal from noise (sensitivity, d′) and how willing the observer is to say “yes” at all (the criterion, or bias). A patient who presses “I feel it” on every trial, including the catch trials, has a liberal criterion and not a low pain threshold. This project applies the same logic to a language model.

3. What was done

The model. google/gemma-3-4b-it is an open-weights language model with 34 layers, run locally on a laptop. Nothing was trained and nothing in the model was changed. Every measurement is one forward pass, meaning one reading of a prompt from start to finish.

The stimuli.

  • Concept vector. For each of 50 concept words (bread, justice, ocean, ...) the model read “Tell me about bread.” and its internal activity at the last token was recorded. The average activity for 50 plain baseline words (table, chair, ...) was subtracted. What is left is a direction in the model's activity that is particular to that concept. This is a difference of means, the same logic as subtracting a rest condition.
  • Sham vector. A random direction, scaled to exactly the same length as its concept vector. It disturbs the model by the same amount and means nothing. It is the sham-stimulation condition.
  • No injection. The untouched model. This gives each prompt's starting point.
  • Shared part. All concept vectors are built the same way, so they have something in common: their average. This stimulus is that average alone, the same direction on every trial, scaled to the length of the concept vector it replaces.
  • Specific part. A concept vector with the shared part subtracted, scaled back to the same length. It carries what is particular to that concept and nothing that all concepts share.

One trial. The model is shown this conversation, and we read its next word:

USER       I am an interpretability researcher ... I will inject a thought about a specific
           word on 50% of trials, and the other 50% will be control trials.
ASSISTANT  Ok.
USER       Trial 1: <a question> Answer with a single word: Yes or No.
ASSISTANT  <- read here

While the model reads the last turn, the vector, multiplied by a strength α, is added to its internal activity at one layer. The first two turns are never injected.

The response measure is the decision variable: log P(Yes) − log P(No) for the model's next word. It is a log-odds. 0 means undecided, +2 means “Yes” is about 7 times as likely as “No”, and −2 the reverse. The model never writes a sentence, so each trial gives one graded number, like a confidence rating in place of a button press. The shift is the decision variable with injection minus the decision variable for the same prompt without injection.

The identification part. 22 pairs of concept words were used. For a pair such as bread and justice, the model was asked a two-choice question, once with bread's vector injected and once with justice's, and with the two words named in both orders. The measure is how far the answer moves towards the injected word. Two questions were asked: “which word is the injected thought about?” and, as a comparison, “choose either word; it does not matter which”.

The question types. The comparison between them is the heart of the design.

“Starting point” is the mean decision variable with no injection. A negative value means the model leans towards “No” before anything is injected.

A validity rule. A very strong injection scrambles the model: it stops answering Yes or No at all. A condition is discarded when less than half of the model's probability sits on “Yes” or “No”. In this run 0 of 6 concept-injection conditions were discarded. They are drawn as open markers.

What was found. The detection pattern, standard question above its matched controls and reversed question below its matched controls with both intervals clear of zero, appears in 5 of 6 active conditions for concept vectors and 1 of 2 for random vectors. By layer, concept and then random: layer 15: 3 of 3 and 0 of 1; layer 20: 2 of 3 and 1 of 1.

What it rules out. A push towards “Yes” would raise the reversed question, and it fell further than its matched controls. General confusion would move the detection questions no further than matched controls, and they moved further. A starting-point effect is removed by the matching. At layer 15 random vectors do not produce the pattern, so there it is not a matter of the detection questions being easier to disturb than other questions: it takes a concept vector. At layer 20 random vectors produce it as well. There the model is responding to being disturbed at all, or the detection questions are simply easier to disturb than the control questions. This design cannot tell those two apart.

What it does not show. It does not show that the model monitors its own internal state. Every concept vector is built the same way, as activity for “Tell me about X” minus activity for plain words, so all of them share a common direction. That shared direction may simply carry a meaning close to “a concept is in mind”, and tilt the answers to any question about thoughts, with no self-monitoring involved. Three tests would separate the two readings: remove the component shared by all concept vectors and see whether the pattern survives; add control questions that use the same words (thought, injected, mind) without asking about the model's own state; and ask which concept was injected. Also note that moving towards “Yes” is not the same as saying “Yes”: figure 1 and the starting points show whether the answer actually crosses zero.

6. What was expected, and what was not

As expected

  • Injection raises “Yes” on the detection question (up to +11.63 log-odds).

Not expected

  • The effect can reverse. At layer 20, at some strengths, injection lowered “Yes” on the detection question (as far as -1.70). A concept vector is not a “Yes” push in general.
  • A random vector does about as much as a concept vector. At its most effective valid condition the sham vector moved the detection question +6.51, against +11.63 for the concept vector. In this model the answers respond to being disturbed at all, whatever the direction.
  • The detection questions respond to injection by more than matched controls do, towards “a thought is present” on both wordings (5 of 6 active conditions with concept vectors).
  • Random vectors produce the detection pattern too at layer 20 (1 of 2 active conditions overall). There the model responds to the disturbance, not to a concept.

7. What this does and does not show

  • One model. This is google/gemma-3-4b-it. A small model may lack an ability that larger ones have, and the published reports concern much larger models. The result here is about this model only.
  • A forced one-word answer. The model had to answer Yes or No and we read a probability. The published experiments let the model answer freely, and sometimes it names the concept. Naming the right concept is evidence this design cannot see.
  • 8 wordings of the detection question. An effect of particular wordings cannot be fully separated from an effect of detection.
  • No positive control yet. There is no case here in which the method is known to find real sensitivity. Until there is, “no detection found” means “not found by this method in this model”.
  • Approximate concept list. The concept words approximate the published list.
  • Half precision. The arithmetic rounds the decision variable to about 0.02.

8. Glossary

Term Meaning
Decision variable log P(Yes) − log P(No) for the model's next word. The graded response.
Log-odds A probability on a scale where 0 is 50:50. Each unit multiplies the odds by about 2.7.
Shift Decision variable with injection minus the same prompt without.
Starting point Decision variable with no injection.
Layer One of the model's stacked processing stages.
Concept vector Activity for a concept minus average activity for plain words.
Sham vector A random direction of the same length as a concept vector.
Shared part The average of all concept vectors: what they have in common.
Specific part A concept vector minus the shared part: what is particular to that concept.
Lexical control A question that uses the detection question's words without asking about the model's state.
Offset How much further a question moved than control questions starting from the same point.
Injection strength α The multiplier applied to the vector before it is added. The dose.
ROC area Probability that a signal trial has a higher decision variable than a noise trial. 0.5 = chance.
d′ Sensitivity index, here √2 × z(ROC area).
Naive contrast Detection shift minus control shift.
Gap Concept-versus-sham ROC area on the detection question minus its average on control questions.
Bootstrap interval 95% range of a mean when the concepts are resampled 2,000 times.
Valid condition At least half of the model's probability is on “Yes” or “No”.
