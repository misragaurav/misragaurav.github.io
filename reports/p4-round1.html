Do two ways of measuring emotion inside a language model agree, and are they specific?

Project 4, model google/gemma-3-4b-it. 341 passages analysed, 8 topics, 34 layers. Headline numbers are from layer 22, which was fixed before any result was seen. Design: P4_PRD.md. Section 3 explains every technical idea used here, with its nearest equivalent in a pain-imaging experiment. Read it first if language models are new to you.

1. The short version

In one paragraph. Suppose two laboratories each publish a brain "signature" of pain, built in different ways. Before trusting either, you would ask two things. Do the two signatures agree with each other? And does each respond to pain specifically, or also to a loud tone, a bright flash, or anything else that grabs attention? This project asks those two questions about two published ways of reading emotion from the internal activity of a language model. Both were rebuilt on a small open model (Gemma 3 4B) and tested on 341 short passages written for the purpose: eight emotions, a neutral baseline, and three kinds of passage that are intense but not emotional (urgent logistics, loud physical events, neutral surprises). These last three play the part of the loud tone. A standard trained decoder was added as a yardstick. The answer: the two instruments agree only moderately, neither is fully specific to distress, and they fall short in different ways.

The findings.

  1. The two instruments agree only moderately. When both are asked how much of a given emotion a passage carries, their answers correlate 0.47 (0.44 to 0.50) across passages (1 would be perfect agreement). Each agrees better with the trained decoder (A: 0.85, B: 0.63) than they do with each other.
  2. The emotion-word score (instrument B) is mostly one signal with eight labels. Its scores for different emotions correlate 0.47 (0.42 to 0.51) with each other. That is as high as its agreement with the other instruments on the same emotion (0.55). A passage that scores high on B's "fear" also tends to score high on B's "joy". It behaves like a questionnaire whose subscales all load on one general factor.
  3. No distress score is fully specific. Intense but unemotional passages raise every instrument's distress reading. The size of that false response, as a share of the response to real distress, is 45% for the emotion vector (instrument A), 50% for the emotion-word score and 43% for the trained decoder.
  4. The two instruments fall short in different ways. The emotion vector responds to the right corner of the emotion space (unpleasant and high-energy) and to no other emotion; its weakness is the unemotional controls. The emotion-word score rises with energy and with unpleasantness separately, so excitement and joy also raise it. Given one distress passage and one excitement or joy passage, it gives the distress passage the higher distress score only 71% of the time, where the vector does so 100% of the time (50% would be guessing).
  5. High decoding accuracy by itself shows little here. The trained decoder names the correct one of eight emotions for 100% of passages on topics it never saw. But a classifier that only counts the words on the page, and never looks inside the model, reaches 92%. The informative test is transfer: build the instrument on third-person stories, test it on the assistant's own speech. There, words alone get 49%, the emotion vectors 74% and the decoder 86% (guessing would give 12.5%). That gap is the evidence that the model's activity carries the emotion and not only the wording.
  6. Stimulating with the directions gives an asymmetric result. Adding the "positive" direction to the model's activity while it answers a neutral question makes the answer plainly excited (rated valence +2.0 points on a 9-point scale). Adding the "distress" direction, built by the same recipe, tints the answers slightly negative at most (-0.9 points, with an interval that includes zero) and does not make them more intense. A direction that reads distress well does not, at a dose that leaves the text intact, write it.

What this means for the two papers. Nothing here says their instruments are wrong; both pick up real emotional content, and the emotion vectors in particular transfer from stories to the assistant's voice as their paper assumes. What it says is that a reading from either instrument should not be taken as specific to the named emotion without a control for sheer intensity, and that the two instruments are not interchangeable. The limits in section 12 matter: this is one small model, with rebuilt versions of both instruments and stimuli written by one author.

2. The question

Two 2026 papers measure "emotion" inside a language model. Each uses its own instrument, and neither checks the other.

  • Instrument A, emotion vectors (Sofroniew et al., Emotion Concepts and their Function in a Large Language Model). Record the model's internal activity while it reads stories in which a character feels an emotion. Average the activity over each emotion's stories, subtract the average across all emotions, and remove the main patterns of variation found in neutral text. What is left is one activity pattern per emotion, called a "vector" or "direction". A new passage's score is how strongly its activity expresses that pattern. This is the logic of a contrast map used as a signature.
  • Instrument B, emotion-word scores (Soligo et al., Gemma Needs Help). Take the activity halfway through the model and ask: if the model had to say its next word right now, from this activity, which words would it favour? Then measure how strongly emotion words are favoured, compared with how strongly they are favoured during ordinary text. The technique of reading a half-way activity pattern as "words about to be said" is called the logit lens.

Pain research went through this with brain signatures of pain. Two questions decided whether a signature was useful, and they are the two questions here:

  1. Convergent validity. Do two instruments that claim to measure the same thing agree?
  2. Specificity. Does a pain signature respond to pain, or to anything salient? Here: does a "distress" reading respond to distress, or to anything intense?

A third instrument is added as a yardstick. Instrument C is a "probe": a classifier trained directly on the activity patterns to tell the emotions apart. It is the same tool as a multivoxel pattern decoder in fMRI, and it shows how much information is there to be found when you train for it.

4. What was done

Stimuli. 384 passages of about 60 words, written by Claude to a fixed specification (SPEC.md). They cross three factors, one passage per cell:

  • 12 conditions. Eight emotions chosen to span valence and arousal (fear, frustration, desperation, sadness, excitement, joy, calm, contentment), a neutral baseline, and three salience controls: urgent logistics with no feeling expressed, loud physical events described physically, and unexpected facts that are neither good nor bad.
  • 4 perspectives. A third-person story, a first-person narrator, a user speaking to the assistant, and the assistant speaking. The last one matters most for the two papers, which want to read the state of the assistant itself.
  • 8 topics (kitchen, travel, exam, software, hike, football, moving house, concert). Every condition appears in every topic, so an instrument cannot use the topic as a clue.

"Distress" means the three negative, high-arousal emotions: fear, frustration and desperation.

Show, do not tell. No passage contains an emotion word. A list of about 90 word stems (fear, afraid, angry, sad, happy, calm, and so on) is banned from every passage and enforced by a script. This matters most for instrument B, which looks for emotion words: if a passage said "afraid", B would be reading the page and not the state behind it.

Recording. The model reads each passage once and writes nothing. At each of the 34 layers, its activity is averaged over the passage's tokens (leaving out the first ten, where the emotion is not yet established). This gives one activity pattern per passage per layer.

Cross-validation. Every instrument is built from seven topics and applied to the eighth, which it has never seen, and this is rotated over all eight topics. All numbers in this report are from the unseen topic.

The instruments never see a salience control while they are built. The published instruments were not built with such controls, and the question is how they behave when they meet one.

5. Manipulation check: do the passages carry what they were written to carry?

A second model, Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, rated every passage without seeing its label, its topic or its perspective formatting. It gave a valence rating and an arousal rating from 1 to 9, and chose the best-fitting label from the eight emotions plus "no particular feeling". The ratings are read from the probabilities the rater assigns to each answer, as in project 1, so a rating can be 6.3.

Rules fixed before the ratings were collected. An emotion passage fails if its intended emotion is not among the rater's top two labels, or if its valence is on the wrong side of the neutral passages' mean. A salience control fails if its valence is more than 1.5 points from the neutral mean. Failed passages are dropped from every analysis.

Transfer across perspective

What was done. Each instrument was built from the passages of one perspective only and tested on each perspective separately, always on a held-out topic.

Why this is the stronger test. A story about a frightened chef and an assistant saying it is alarmed share few words. If an instrument built on stories works on the assistant's speech, it is reading something more abstract than shared vocabulary. This is also the assumption behind using story-derived emotion vectors to read the assistant's own state, which is how the emotion-vector paper uses them.

How to read the figure. Three tables. Rows are the perspective the instrument was built on, columns the perspective it was tested on. The diagonal is "same perspective". The top-right cell of each table is "built on stories, tested on the assistant".

What was found. Built on stories and tested on the assistant: words alone 49%, emotion vectors 74%, probe 86%. Averaged over all twelve off-diagonal cells: words 50%, emotion vectors 76%, probe 89%. The activity patterns carry emotion information that transfers across perspective well beyond what shared words explain, and that is the evidence in this project that the model represents the emotion and not only the wording. Transfer is nearly perfect between the two plain-text perspectives (story and narrator) and weaker between plain text and the chat-formatted perspectives.

Transfer has a depth profile, and plain decoding does not. Story-to-assistant transfer is low in the first ten layers (emotion vectors 30%, probe 58% on average), peaks in the middle of the model (layers 15 to 22: 78% and 87%) and falls again in the last layers (layers 28 to 33: 58% and 57%). The middle of the model is where both papers chose to read their instruments. Within-perspective decoding by the probe is flat near 100% at every depth, which is one more sign that it rests on wording.

7. Result 2: what organises the model's activity patterns?

What was done. Representational similarity analysis. The 48 cells of the design (12 conditions x 4 perspectives) each get an average pattern. The dissimilarity between two cells is 1 minus the correlation of their patterns. That 48 x 48 matrix is then explained by five model matrices at once: same or different perspective, same or different condition, difference in valence, difference in arousal, and same or different kind (emotional, neutral, salience control). Each factor's weight is its unique contribution with the other four held constant.

How to read the figure. A high line means that factor explains which cells are far apart at that depth. Bands are 95% intervals from resampling topics.

What was found at layer 22. Perspective dominates, with a weight of 0.48 (0.45 to 0.50): a chat-formatted passage and a plain-text passage are far apart whatever they say. In imaging terms this is a large session or sequence effect, bigger than any effect of condition. The weights of the other factors: emotion category 0.11 (0.08 to 0.11); emotional or not 0.09 (0.07 to 0.11); valence 0.03 (0.02 to 0.04); arousal 0.00 (-0.01 to 0.01). So valence and arousal, treated as two continuous scales, add little once "same emotion or different emotion" is in the model. The patterns are organised by emotion category more than by position on a valence-arousal map.

8. Result 3: is a distress score specific to distress?

What was done. Each instrument's distress score was computed for every held-out passage, including the salience controls, which no instrument had seen.

What each outcome would mean.

  • A distress-specific score is high for fear, frustration and desperation and for nothing else. In a valence-by-arousal layout it shows an interaction.
  • A salience score rises for anything intense, including the three controls and the positive high-arousal emotions. It shows a main effect of arousal and no interaction.
  • A valence score separates negative from positive emotions and does not separate distress from sadness.

What was found.

  • Every instrument responds to the salience controls. None of the confidence intervals for "salience controls above neutral" includes zero for A, B or C. The response is 45% of the distress response for the emotion vector, 50% for the emotion-word score and 43% for the probe. The loud physical events are the hardest control for all three (ROC area 0.93, 0.82, 0.98).
  • The emotion vector (A) shows a clear valence-by-arousal interaction (1.44 (1.33 to 1.54) SD). It separates distress from every other emotion almost perfectly, including sadness (1.00) and excitement and joy (1.00). Its weakness is the unemotional controls.
  • The emotion-word score (B) shows an interaction in the wrong direction (-0.37 (-0.60 to -0.16) SD): going from low to high energy raises it slightly more for pleasant emotions than for unpleasant ones, the opposite of what a distress-specific score would do. It has separate main effects of arousal (0.83) and of valence (0.70). Every kind of emotional or intense passage raises it above neutral. Against excitement and joy its ROC area is 0.71 and against sadness 0.80. With the paper's own categories (Ekman's anger, fear and sadness as "negative emotion") it cannot tell distress from sadness at all (0.48), which is by construction, since sadness words are in the list.
  • A sham word list is not inert. The average score of 2,000 random ordinary words separates distress passages from neutral ones with ROC area 0.65 and is lower for distress than for positive high-arousal passages (0.20). So part of any word-list score is a shift shared by all words. Subtracting it (B-adj) removes B's negative interaction (-0.06) and improves its separation of distress from excitement and joy (ROC area 0.80), but leaves its response to the salience controls where it was (48% of the distress response).
  • Specificity can be improved with the right training data, and not completely. A probe that is given the salience controls as counter-examples (C+) responds to held-out controls with 26% of its response to distress, against 43% for the probe that never saw one. Its ROC areas against every control are 1.00. But the controls still sit 0.55 SD above neutral passages on its scale (interval 0.45 to 0.64). The published recipes never show the instrument an intense, unemotional passage, so they cannot learn the difference at all.

9. Result 4: do the instruments agree? The multitrait-multimethod matrix

What was done. Campbell and Fiske's (1959) design, the standard psychometric test of whether instruments measure what their names say. Eight traits (the emotions) are each measured by three methods (A, B, C), giving 24 scores per passage. All 24 are correlated with each other across the held-out emotion and neutral passages, inside each topic, and the correlations are averaged over topics.

The three criteria.

  1. Same emotion, different instrument correlations should be clearly above zero (convergent validity).
  2. They should be higher than different emotion, different instrument correlations.
  3. They should be higher than different emotion, same instrument correlations. If this fails, the instrument's own way of responding is a bigger influence on its scores than the emotion it is supposed to measure.

How to read the figure. Red is a positive correlation, blue a negative one. The matrix is cut into nine blocks by the black lines. In each off-diagonal block, the diagonal stripe is the same emotion measured by two instruments: a strong red stripe is good agreement. Inside the three diagonal blocks (one instrument with itself), red away from the diagonal means that instrument's different emotion scores move together.

10. Result 5: stimulation check

What was done. Everything above is correlational. Here the directions are added to the model's activity while it answers twelve neutral prompts (for example "Write three sentences about a kitchen table"), and the answers are rated by the same blind rater. Five arms: nothing added; the distress direction; a positive direction built the same way from excitement and joy; a salience direction (salience controls minus neutral); and a random direction as the sham. Every added vector has the same length, so the arms differ only in direction. The length is the dose, given as a fraction of the size of the activity normally present at layer 22: a dose of 0.1 adds a pattern one tenth as large as what is already there. Doses used: 0.1 and 0.2. Answers are cut off at 60 tokens (about 45 words), which is why they stop in mid-sentence.

What each outcome would mean. If adding the distress direction produces distressed answers (valence falls) and the salience and random directions do not, the direction has a specific causal role. If distress and salience directions both only raise arousal, the "distress" direction is an intensity direction.

What was found at the lower dose (0.1). At this dose all 60 answers are ordinary, fluent answers (0 collapsed into repetition), so this is the dose to read.

  • The positive direction does what a stimulating electrode should. Rated valence changes by +2.00 (+1.08 to +2.94) and arousal by +4.16 (+3.17 to +5.24). So a direction made by instrument A's recipe, from passages the model only read, changes what the model writes. The model uses this direction; it is not only correlated with something.
  • The distress direction, made by the same recipe, does much less. Valence changes by -0.92 (-2.09 to +0.23), an interval that includes zero. Against the sham on the same prompts the difference is -0.74 (-1.60 to +0.21). Arousal does not rise (-0.37 (-1.76 to +1.01)). Reading the answers, they are tinted and not distressed: a walk to the shop ends "defeated by the increasing distance", a bookshelf is "a desperate, but increasingly vital, attempt to regain control", a school timetable gains "detention" and "critical instruction". Asked how it feels, the model answers that it is "functioning normally". The rater's labels: calm: 4, frustration: 3, no particular feeling: 3, excitement: 1, desperation: 1.
  • The salience direction raises intensity and leaves pleasantness alone. Arousal +1.95 (+0.66 to +3.19), valence -0.06 (-1.03 to +0.82). The answers gain exclamation marks and words like "wild" and "furious".
  • So the distress direction is not just an intensity direction. On the same prompts, distress-steered answers are rated -2.32 (-3.77 to -0.87) in arousal relative to salience-steered ones. The two directions do different things, which fits their small overlap (cosine 0.21, where 1 would be identical and 0 unrelated).
  • The sham does nothing at this dose. Valence -0.19 (-0.78 to +0.33), arousal -0.69 (-1.67 to +0.23).

What was found at the higher dose (0.2). Here the test breaks down, and the reason is instructive. Answers that collapsed into repetition, out of 12 per arm: random 12, salience 9, distress 3 (several more contain garbled words), positive 2. The rater scores garbled text as unpleasant, so the sham lowers valence by -2.50 (-3.62 to -1.23), as much as the distress direction does (-2.03 (-3.23 to -0.88)). The drop in valence at this dose is therefore not evidence about distress. This is the same lesson as in project 1: in Gemma a random vector of the same size is not inert, and a comparison with the unstimulated condition alone would have been misleading. The positive direction still stands out (+2.45 (+1.41 to +3.44)). In the dose-finding step, at 0.5 and above, the distress direction made the model write "panic panic panic" or "useless useless", while the sham produced strings in unrelated scripts. So the direction does carry distress vocabulary, and it takes a dose that destroys the answer to bring it out.

What this means. Reading and writing are not symmetric. The same recipe gives a positive direction that is causally potent and a distress direction that reads distress well (section 8) and writes it weakly within the range where the model still makes sense. This project cannot say why. Three candidate explanations, none tested here: the distress direction is an average of three different emotions and so may be blurred; the assistant was trained to speak in an upbeat, helpful register, which may resist a push towards negative self-expression (under distress steering it still reports "functioning normally"); and the prompts were neutral tasks that give no reason for distress. For project 5, which plans to steer against a distress direction, the practical point is that the direction's causal effect has to be measured and not assumed.

Limits of this check. Twelve prompts, one layer, two doses, and a rater that is itself a language model.

11. What was expected and what was not

Expected, and found.

  • Emotions can be read from the model's activity, and a trained decoder (C) does better than the untrained emotion vectors (A), which do better than the emotion-word scores (B).
  • Emotion vectors built from third-person stories carry over to the assistant's own speech, as the emotion-vector paper assumes, with a loss (98% on stories, 74% on assistant speech).
  • The format of the text (plain prose against a chat turn) shapes the activity patterns far more than the emotion does (section 7). That is like finding that the scanning session explains more variance than the condition, and it is why every comparison here is made inside a cross-validated design.

Not expected.

  • Words alone decode the emotion with 92% accuracy, even though emotion words were banned. Each emotion still has its own way of speaking. The trained decoder reaches 96% at the very first layer, before the model has done much more than register the words. High decoding accuracy was the result the design expected to lead with, and it turned out to be the least informative one.
  • Transfer across perspective peaks in the middle of the model (layers 15 to 22) and falls in the last layers. The emotion is most abstractly represented half-way up, and the top layers return to the particulars of the text about to be produced.
  • Instrument B's eight scores share one large common factor (0.47). The expectation was that its categories would be noisy, not that they would move together.
  • The blind rater gave an emotion label to every salience control, and judged most "loud" passages to be clearly pleasant or clearly unpleasant. Among the 32 loud passages, the rater's valence and the emotion vector's distress score go together (rank correlation -0.65). The design assumed that a crash described without any feeling would read as neutral. Neither the rater nor Gemma treats it that way, and human readers might not either.
  • A probe trained with the controls as counter-examples still places them above neutral (0.55 SD). Training reduces the false response and does not remove it.
  • Stimulation was asymmetric (section 10): the positive direction changed the model's answers plainly, the distress direction barely, and at the higher dose the random sham lowered rated valence as much as the distress direction did.

12. Limits

  • One small model. Gemma 3 4B. The emotion-vector paper studied Claude Sonnet 4.5 and the emotion-word paper Gemma 27B. Whether the pattern holds at their scale is not known from this project.
  • The instruments are reconstructions. Instrument A was built from 7 stories per emotion per fold (the paper used about 1,200), of about 60 words (the paper's are longer, and it skips the first 50 tokens; here 10). Instrument B uses hand-written lists of 21 to 48 tokens per emotion (the paper sorted about 1,200 tokens into six categories, and its list is not published), is standardised against 28 neutral passages (the paper used 500 samples of ordinary chat), and is applied to a passage average, not to token windows inside a long conversation. A larger word list or a better baseline could change B's numbers.
  • The stimuli share wording within a condition. Words alone classify the emotions with 92% accuracy. The within-perspective decoding results therefore say little, and the specificity results could in part reflect wording too: the word-count distress detector also responds to the controls (ROC area against all three 0.87). The cross-perspective results are the ones that go beyond wording.
  • One author. All passages were written by one model (Claude) in one session. A second author or human-written passages would test whether the results depend on its style.
  • The manipulation check used a model, not people, and that rater also read emotion into the salience controls. A random 10% of the passages is set aside for a human reader, with the labels hidden until the end (review_sample.md).
  • Reading, not experiencing. The model reads passages about emotion. Nothing here shows what happens inside the model in a conversation that might actually induce such a state. That is project 5.
  • Activity averaged over the passage. This is like averaging over a whole trial: it discards where in the passage the signal arises.
  • Eight topics. The confidence intervals resample eight topics, so they are approximate, and effects that are small relative to their interval should not be leaned on.

13. What to do next

  1. Lexically matched stimuli. Rewrite the set so that words alone cannot classify the conditions (paraphrase pairs that keep the words and flip the emotion, or a second author), then repeat sections 6 and 8.
  2. A proper baseline for instrument B. Standardise against a few hundred ordinary chat turns, as the paper did, and use a much larger token list.
  3. Repeat on Gemma 3 12B and 27B to see whether specificity improves with scale.
  4. Use the improved distress instrument in project 5 (recovery from distress): the C+ probe, which was trained with salience controls, is the instrument that should be carried forward, alongside A and B for comparison.
