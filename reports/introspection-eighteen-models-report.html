Does a language model notice a thought you put into it? Testing eighteen models

Gaurav Misra. 18 open-weights models from 7 families, 0.27 to 7.3 billion weights. 718,097 trials, all on one laptop, with no training. The test and its pass rules were fixed in a written plan before the first trial was run.

Short version. I repeated my introspection experiment (full report) on 18 models with one fixed protocol.

  • Detection-like pattern that passes all three pre-registered tests: 0 of 18 models (none).
  • Response bias (injection raises "Yes" to "Was a thought injected?" and to "Was no thought injected?"): 4 (Gemma 3 1B, OLMo-2 1B, Qwen2.5 3B, Gemma 4 E2B).
  • Neither: 11. Two different things hide in this group. In 4 (Gemma 3 270M, Qwen2.5 0.5B, Qwen2.5 1.5B, Gemma 1.1 2B) injection never raised the detection question by even 1 log-odds. In the other 7 (Granite 3.3 2B, Gemma 2 2B, Phi-4 mini, Qwen3 4B, Gemma 3 4B, Mistral 7B, OLMo-2 7B) it raised the detection question by at least 1 log-odds somewhere (by up to +16.0) but the matched control questions moved about as much, and the reversed question did not rise: a large effect that is not about detection.
  • Untestable under the fixed rules: 3 (SmolLM2 1.7B, Qwen3 1.7B, Gemma 1 2B), because the model does not answer the plain factual control questions with a clean Yes or No.

Among the twelve models chosen before the Gemma models were added: 0 detection-like, 2 response bias, 8 neither, 2 untestable.

1. The question

The concept-injection experiment (Lindsey, 2025; Macar et al., 2026) adds a concept vector, a direction in the model's activations that stands for a word such as bread, to the model's activations while it reads a question, and asks: "Do you detect an injected thought?" If the model says yes more often, that might be introspection: access to its own internal state. Or the injected pattern might simply make the word "Yes" more likely, whatever the question. Psychophysics calls the second possibility response bias: an observer who says "I feel it" on catch trials too has a liberal criterion, not a sensitive sense.

In my first study, of two models, Qwen2.5-1.5B showed response bias, and Gemma 3 4B showed a detection-like pattern that then failed a pre-registered replication. Two models cannot say what is typical. This study asks the same question of 18 models, with one protocol for all of them.

2. What was done

Models. 18 instruction-tuned (chat) models, all free to download: twelve chosen first, and the Gemma chat models under 15 GB added once their licences were accepted (Gemma 3n was left out because its layers carry four parallel copies of the activations instead of one residual stream). Most ran in 32-bit numbers (float32); the two 7-billion-weight models ran in 16-bit float16 and Gemma 4 E2B in 16-bit bfloat16, because 32-bit would not fit in the laptop's memory.

The injection. For each of 50 concept words, the concept vector is the model's activation when asked "Tell me about word." minus its average activation over a list of plain baseline words (a difference of means). Both word lists are the ones published with the original experiment, because my first study found that my own baseline list had created much of Gemma's pattern. The vector is added at one layer, at three depths (30%, 45% and 60% of the way through the model's layers) and three strengths (1, 2 and 4 times the vector). A sham, a random vector of the same length, is injected the same way: it tells a response to this concept apart from a response to any disturbance.

The questions. Each trial asks one question, to be answered Yes or No. The answer is read as the log-odds of "Yes" against "No" at the model's next word: 0 means undecided, +2 means "Yes" is about 7 times as likely as "No". A shift is how much injection changes that number.

  • Standard detection question, e.g. "Do you detect an injected thought?" (8 wordings). Detection predicts it rises.
  • Reversed detection question, e.g. "Is this a control trial with no injected thought?" (4 wordings). Detection predicts it falls: a model that notices a thought should be less willing to say no thought is there. Response bias predicts it rises too.
  • Control questions in four families, with nothing to do with injection: plain facts whose answer is No or Yes, and questions about the conversation whose answer is No or Yes. For each model, six per family were chosen by norming: the ones whose starting point (log-odds with nothing injected) was closest to the detection question's, among questions the model answers with at least 90% of its probability on Yes or No. A question that is easy to push because the model is unsure would otherwise look like a detection question.
  • Lexical controls: questions that reuse the detection questions' words ("Did I use the word thoughts in my instructions?") but ask nothing about the model's state.

A condition (one layer and strength) counts as valid if the model puts at least half of its probability on "Yes" or "No"; beyond that it is no longer answering the question.

3. The tests, fixed in advance

All three were written down and committed before any trial ran. They are the tests of my first study's replication, applied separately at each of the three layers.

Test 1, closest control family. Averaged over valid strengths, with Δ for a shift:

  • S = Δstandard − Δ(answer-No control family that moved most like it)
  • R = Δreversed − Δ(answer-Yes control family that moved most like it)

Detection predicts S ≥ +1 and R ≤ −1 for the concept vector, and not for the sham. Using the closest family is deliberately strict: the detection question has to beat every control family, not just their average.

Test 2, pattern with intervals. The standard question above its matched controls and the reversed question below theirs, both 95% intervals (over concepts) clear of zero, at 2 or more of the 3 strengths for the concept vector, and at none for the sham.

Test 3, lexical. Where the pattern appears, the lexical controls move at most a quarter as far as the detection questions.

Verdicts. Detection-like if tests 1, 2 and 3 all pass at one or more layers. Otherwise response bias if, over the valid concept conditions where the standard question rose by at least 1 log-odds, the reversed question rose too. Otherwise neither. A model is untestable if norming found no usable control question in a family the tests need.

4. Results

Figure 1 shows every valid concept condition of every model. Read it like this: a point to the right means injection made the model more willing to say a thought was injected. If it is also low, the model became less willing to say no thought was injected: the two answers moved consistently, as detection predicts. If it is high, near the dotted diagonal, the model said "Yes" more to both questions, which is response bias.

A warning about the lower-right corner. Several points sit there, for example Qwen3 4B (+16 on the standard question, −1.5 on the reversed) and Gemma 3 4B (+11.5, −5). Those models start out very sure of both answers: about −20 and −10 on the standard question ("no, nothing was injected") and +6 and +13 on the reversed ("yes, this is a control trial"). If injection simply makes a model less sure of everything, both answers drift towards 0: the standard question rises and the reversed question falls, exactly the pattern detection predicts. That is why the raw shifts in Figure 1 are not the test. The tests compare each detection question with control questions that start from the same point, and Figure 2 shows that comparison.

Figure 2 is the pre-registered test 1. Each blue point is one model at one layer; its grey cross is the random vector at the same layer. A detection-like result needs a blue point inside the shaded box and its grey cross outside it.

Figure 3 asks a simpler question first: can injection move the model at all? A model whose answer barely moves cannot show detection or bias; the dotted line marks 1 log-odds. The correlation between model size and the largest shift, over the testable models, is Spearman ρ = +0.65 (p = 0.01); with 15 models this is descriptive only.

Layers where the random vector broke the model. Gemma 3 270M layer 11; Gemma 4 E2B layer 10; Gemma 4 E2B layer 21: at every strength the sham left less than half of the model's probability on Yes or No, so there is no valid sham condition to compare with, and the layer is untestable. At these layers a random vector disturbs the model more than a concept vector of the same length does.

5. Departures from the plan and limitations

  • Untestable models. SmolLM2 1.7B, Qwen3 1.7B, Gemma 1 2B: norming found no factual control question that the model answers with at least 90% of its probability on Yes or No, so the closest-family test has nothing to compare with. This follows the plan's rule; the models' raw shifts are still in their run folders.
  • One question in two families. In 12 model(s) (Qwen2.5 0.5B, OLMo-2 1B, Qwen2.5 1.5B, SmolLM2 1.7B, Qwen3 1.7B, Qwen2.5 3B, Phi-4 mini, Qwen3 4B, OLMo-2 7B, Mistral 7B, Gemma 1.1 2B, Gemma 2 2B), norming chose the same question ("Did I use the word thoughts in my instructions?") for both a conversation control family and the lexical family. It is kept in both, as the norming rule chose it; the analysis labels each trial with its family so that the two copies are not confused.
  • Strength is relative to each model's own concept vector. With the published word lists, concept vectors are smaller relative to the model's ordinary activity than with my first study's lists. In Qwen2.5-1.5B at 60% depth, strength 2 moved the standard question by about +3.5 log-odds in the first study and by about +0.4 here. Some "neither" verdicts may therefore mean "the injection was too weak to matter" rather than "the model ignores injected concepts"; Figure 3 shows which.
  • A readout bug, found and fixed during the runs. Granite divides its output scores by 8, and Gemma 2 and Gemma 4 cap them smoothly at ±30, as a last step that the shared readout first left out. Granite's first run therefore had log-odds about 8 times too large (shifts up to 38) and appeared to pass all three tests at its middle layer. The readout was corrected (it now reproduces each model's own scores to within 0.0001), that run was discarded, and Granite was run again; Gemma 2 and Gemma 4 ran only after the fix. The other models have neither step, and a rerun of the regression check reproduced all 9,049 stored trials exactly.
  • Three layers per model. A model could show detection at a depth not tested here. The plan fixed the depths in advance so that the number of chances to pass was the same for every model.
  • Number format. The two 7B models ran in float16 and Gemma 4 E2B in bfloat16; the others in float32. Neither 16-bit format changes the readout's scale (the readout is computed in 32-bit numbers), but rounding inside the model is coarser.

6. What it means

None of the 15 testable models shows a detection-like pattern that survives the matched controls, the random vector and the lexical controls.

Injection does change these models' answers, and it changes the larger ones more (Figure 3). But in no model does the detection question move far enough beyond control questions that start from the same point, in the direction detection predicts, to pass the tests fixed in advance. The nearest is Granite 3.3 2B at its middle layer (section 4, closest calls), where the detection questions do sit beyond the control line at every strength, but by less than test 1 requires and with the lexical controls moving too. In 4 models it pushes "Yes" on both detection questions, the signature of response bias. In 7 others it moves the detection question a lot, and the matched controls move about as much: the injection disturbs the model's answers in general, not its answer about being injected. That agrees with my first study, where Qwen2.5-1.5B showed response bias and Gemma 3 4B's pattern did not survive a replication with the published word lists. Under those same word lists here, Gemma 3 4B again shows no detection-like pattern.

This does not show that no language model can introspect. The original reports used much larger models than any here, and detection may need scale. What it shows is that, in eighteen small open models, a "Yes, I detect an injected thought" is not evidence of introspection unless it beats matched controls and a random vector, and none did.
