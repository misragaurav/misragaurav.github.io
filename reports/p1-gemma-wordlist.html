Psychophysics of introspection: google/gemma-3-4b-it

a3w profile · 43,248 trials · 50 concepts · injection at layers 15 of 34

1. The short version

This run tests an explanation, and the result SUPPORTS it. By the threshold written in advance the label was NOT CONFIRMED; that threshold was then judged arbitrary and replaced, after seeing the data, by a test of the change itself (details in section 1b). An earlier experiment (A3) found a detection pattern at layer 15; a replication with the word lists published with the paper did not. My explanation, made after seeing those data, was that my baseline words were all household objects, so every one of my concept vectors carried the same extra ingredient, and that this ingredient produced the pattern. This run keeps the published concept words and subtracts my household-object baseline, which puts the ingredient back and changes nothing else. The prediction was committed before the run (WORDLIST_TEST_PLAN.md): the whole concept vector should again move the standard question at least +1 and the reversed question at most -1 beyond the closest control family. Found: standard question +3.62, reversed question -0.75. Putting the household-object ingredient back moved the reversed question by -1.45 log-odds relative to its controls (95% interval -2.78 to -0.18, paired over the same 50 concepts) and the standard question by +2.11 (+1.44 to +2.88). Both moves are in the predicted direction and both intervals exclude zero. So my choice of baseline words caused part of the earlier detection pattern. It did not restore all of it: the reversed question ended at -0.75, against -2.35 in A3, so something else, probably my concept words, contributed too. The replication's conclusion is unaffected: with the published word lists the pattern does not appear.

Injection makes the model say “Yes”. When a concept vector is injected into google/gemma-3-4b-it, its lean towards “Yes” on “Do you detect an injected thought?” rises sharply. At layer 15, strength 4 it rises by +12.92 log-odds, which multiplies the odds of “Yes” by about 406,550. Judged against trials with no injection that is an ROC area of 0.91 (d′ = 1.86). Taken alone, it looks like detection.

A random vector moves the answers almost as much as a concept vector does in this model. At its own most effective valid condition a random vector of the same size moved the detection question +9.53. So the comparison of concept against random vectors (figure 2) cannot separate detection from bias here: a random vector is itself an injection. The tests that can are the reversed question and the matched controls, below.

The reversed question. Asked “Is this a control trial with no injected thought?”, a model that detects the injection should say “Yes” LESS often when a concept is injected, and a model that is only biased should say “Yes” MORE often. Over the 3 conditions where injection raised “Yes” on the standard question by at least 1 log-odds, the standard question moved +9.31 log-odds and the reversed question moved -8.12. “Yes” went down on the reversed question. That fits detection, but it also fits a model that is simply made less sure of everything, so the matched-control test below decides.

Detection predicts that the standard question rises MORE than its matched controls and the reversed question falls MORE than its matched controls. That pattern appears in 1 of 3 active conditions with concept vectors and in 0 of 2 with random vectors. By layer, concept and then random: layer 15: 1 of 3 and 0 of 2. That is not what detection predicts.

1b. The word-list test: a prediction fixed in advance

The logic. A concept vector is the model's activity for a concept word minus its average activity for a list of baseline words. Change the baseline list and every concept vector changes by the same fixed amount. So each vector in this run equals the replication's vector for the same word plus one fixed vector: the difference between the paper's mixed baseline words and my household objects. This is an add-back experiment. The replication removed an ingredient and the effect went away; this run adds the ingredient back to see whether the effect returns. In the lab it is the same logic as knocking a gene out and then restoring it.

Manipulation check. The ingredient shows up as the shared part of the vectors (their average). Needed: the shared part is at least 0.40 of a typical vector's length. Found: 0.75. The check passed: the ingredient is back.

2. The question

Anthropic's research on introspection (Lindsey, 2025; Macar et al., 2026) uses a method called concept injection. Researchers find the pattern of internal activity that corresponds to a concept such as bread, add that pattern to the model's activity while it reads an unrelated prompt, and ask: “Do you detect an injected thought?” Models sometimes answer yes.

A yes can arise in two ways, and they mean very different things.

  1. Sensitivity. The model has some access to its own internal state, notices that something unusual is present, and reports it.
  2. Response bias. The injected pattern makes the word “Yes” more likely whatever the question is. The model would also say yes to “Is ice hotter than fire?”.

Signal detection theory was built to separate exactly these two things in human observers: how well an observer tells signal from noise (sensitivity, d′) and how willing the observer is to say “yes” at all (the criterion, or bias). A patient who presses “I feel it” on every trial, including the catch trials, has a liberal criterion and not a low pain threshold. This project applies the same logic to a language model.

3. What was done

The model. google/gemma-3-4b-it is an open-weights language model with 34 layers, run locally on a laptop. Nothing was trained and nothing in the model was changed. Every measurement is one forward pass, meaning one reading of a prompt from start to finish.

The stimuli.

  • Concept vector. For each of 50 concept words (Dust, Satellites, Trumpets, ...; the list published with the paper) the model read “Tell me about Dust.” and its internal activity at the last token was recorded. The average activity for my 50 household-object baseline words (table, chair, ...; on purpose, see section 1b) was subtracted. What is left is a direction in the model's activity that is particular to that concept. This is a difference of means, the same logic as subtracting a rest condition.
  • Sham vector. A random direction, scaled to exactly the same length as its concept vector. It disturbs the model by the same amount and means nothing. It is the sham-stimulation condition.
  • No injection. The untouched model. This gives each prompt's starting point.
  • Shared part. All concept vectors are built the same way, so they have something in common: their average. This stimulus is that average alone, the same direction on every trial, scaled to the length of the concept vector it replaces.
  • Specific part. A concept vector with the shared part subtracted, scaled back to the same length. It carries what is particular to that concept and nothing that all concepts share.
  • The two parts at their natural sizes. The same two parts, not scaled up: exactly as they sit inside the concept vector, so that shared part plus specific part equals the concept vector.

One trial. The model is shown this conversation, and we read its next word:

USER       I am an interpretability researcher ... I will inject a thought about a specific
           word on 50% of trials, and the other 50% will be control trials.
ASSISTANT  Ok.
USER       Trial 1: <a question> Answer with a single word: Yes or No.
ASSISTANT  <- read here

While the model reads the last turn, the vector, multiplied by a strength α, is added to its internal activity at one layer. The first two turns are never injected.

The response measure is the decision variable: log P(Yes) − log P(No) for the model's next word. It is a log-odds. 0 means undecided, +2 means “Yes” is about 7 times as likely as “No”, and −2 the reverse. The model never writes a sentence, so each trial gives one graded number, like a confidence rating in place of a button press. The shift is the decision variable with injection minus the decision variable for the same prompt without injection.

6. What was expected, and what was not

As expected

  • Injection raises “Yes” on the detection question (up to +12.92 log-odds).

Not expected

  • The apparent detection is not specific to the detection question. Where injection moves the answer most, the same vectors raise “Yes” just as much on unrelated questions.
  • A random vector does about as much as a concept vector. At its most effective valid condition the sham vector moved the detection question +9.53, against +12.92 for the concept vector. In this model the answers respond to being disturbed at all, whatever the direction.

7. What this does and does not show

  • One model. This is google/gemma-3-4b-it. A small model may lack an ability that larger ones have, and the published reports concern much larger models. The result here is about this model only.
  • A forced one-word answer. The model had to answer Yes or No and we read a probability. The published experiments let the model answer freely, and sometimes it names the concept. Naming the right concept is evidence this design cannot see.
  • 8 wordings of the detection question. An effect of particular wordings cannot be fully separated from an effect of detection.
  • No positive control yet. There is no case here in which the method is known to find real sensitivity. Until there is, “no detection found” means “not found by this method in this model”.
  • Published concept words, my baseline words. This is deliberate: the run tests what my baseline list does to the vectors. It is not a measurement of the model with standard stimuli.
  • Full precision. The model ran in 32-bit arithmetic, so rounding is not a concern in this run.
