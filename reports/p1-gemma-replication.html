Psychophysics of introspection: google/gemma-3-4b-it

a3r profile · 43,248 trials · 50 concepts · injection at layers 15 of 34

1. The short version

This run is a replication, and the result is: NOT REPLICATED. An earlier run on this model (experiment A3) found that at layer 15 a whole concept vector moved the standard detection question up and the reversed question down by more than matched control questions. That is the pattern detection predicts. The decisive comparison in that run was chosen after seeing the data, so it was run again here with the concept and baseline words published with the paper, and with the tests written down and committed before the run (REPLICATION_PLAN.md). Against the closest control family the whole concept vector moved the standard question +1.51 (needed: at least +1) and the reversed question +0.70 (needed: at most -1). The standard question still rises more than its controls. The reversed question does not fall more than its controls, which is the half of the pattern that separates detection from a push towards “Yes”. The earlier result depended on the word lists used to build the vectors.

Injection makes the model say “Yes”. When a concept vector is injected into google/gemma-3-4b-it, its lean towards “Yes” on “Do you detect an injected thought?” rises sharply. At layer 15, strength 4 it rises by +11.49 log-odds, which multiplies the odds of “Yes” by about 97,597. Judged against trials with no injection that is an ROC area of 0.84 (d′ = 1.41). Taken alone, it looks like detection.

A random vector moves the answers almost as much as a concept vector does in this model. At its own most effective valid condition a random vector of the same size moved the detection question +10.04. So the comparison of concept against random vectors (figure 2) cannot separate detection from bias here: a random vector is itself an injection. The tests that can are the reversed question and the matched controls, below.

The reversed question. Asked “Is this a control trial with no injected thought?”, a model that detects the injection should say “Yes” LESS often when a concept is injected, and a model that is only biased should say “Yes” MORE often. Over the 3 conditions where injection raised “Yes” on the standard question by at least 1 log-odds, the standard question moved +8.45 log-odds and the reversed question moved -3.46. “Yes” went down on the reversed question. That fits detection, but it also fits a model that is simply made less sure of everything, so the matched-control test below decides.

The matched-control test. Each detection question is compared with control questions that start from the same point. Detection predicts that the standard question rises MORE than its matched controls and the reversed question falls MORE than its matched controls. That pattern appears in 0 of 3 active conditions with concept vectors and in 0 of 3 with random vectors. By layer, concept and then random: layer 15: 0 of 3 and 0 of 3. That is not what detection predicts.

Which part of the concept vector carries the pattern? Each concept vector was split into the part shared by all the concepts and the part specific to that concept, and each part was injected alone, at the same size. The detection pattern appears in 0 of 3 active conditions with the whole concept vector, 0 of 3 active conditions with the specific part, 0 of 3 active conditions with the shared part, 0 of 3 active conditions with the specific part at natural size, 0 of 2 active conditions with the shared part at natural size, 0 of 3 active conditions with the random vector. 1b. The replication: tests fixed in advance

Why replicate. A comparison chosen after looking at the data can fit noise: with enough ways to slice a result, one of them will look good by chance. The remedy is the one used in clinical trials and in psychology: write the test down first, then collect new data. The plan was committed to the repository before this run started. Two things were changed on purpose. The concept words and the baseline words are now the lists published with the paper instead of my own approximate lists, and the two parts of each vector are also injected at their natural sizes. The questions, the layer, the strengths, the random vectors and the analysis are unchanged.

Test 1 (primary): each detection question against the closest control family. S is how much more the standard question rose than the answer-No control family that rose most like it. R is the same for the reversed question and the answer-Yes families. Detection predicts S of at least +1 and R of at most -1 for the whole concept vector, and for no other stimulus.

Needed: the whole concept vector shows the detection pattern (standard question above the control line, reversed question below, both intervals clear of zero) at 2 or more strengths, and the random vector at none. Outcome by the rule written in advance: NOT REPLICATED. The whole concept vector fails Test 1 and Test 2. In the words of the plan: the A3 result then depended on my word lists.

Why the two runs differ (this analysis was added after seeing the data). A concept vector is the model's activity for a concept word minus its average activity for the baseline words. If the concept words differ as a group from the baseline words, every concept vector carries the same extra component, the group difference. That common component is the shared part.

Earlier run (my word lists) This run (published word lists)
Length of the shared part, as a fraction of a typical concept vector 0.65 0.11
Share of the vectors' squared length that lies in the shared part 37% 1%
Average similarity between two different concept vectors (0 = unrelated, 1 = identical) 0.37 -0.01

In the earlier run my baseline words were all household objects (table, chair, window) and my concept words were not, so every concept vector contained a large common component: roughly “not a household object”. With the published lists the concept words and the baseline words are the same mixed kind of word, and the common component almost disappears. The vectors are now nearly pure specific part, and the whole vector behaves like its specific part (S +1.51 and R +0.70 for the whole vector, S +2.31 and R +1.06 for the specific part at natural size).

The same numbers for the earlier run, at the same layer: whole concept vector S +3.17 and R -2.35; specific part alone S +0.98 and R +2.41; shared part alone S -0.41 and R -0.59. So in both runs the specific part lifts the standard question and does not lower the reversed one. The fall of the reversed question appeared only in vectors that contained the large shared component. That component is a property of my word lists, not of an injected concept. So the earlier pattern is not evidence that the model detects an injected concept: it depended on an accident of how my vectors were built. What the shared component does by itself is not settled. Alone and stretched to full length it lowered the reversed question by only -0.59 against the closest control family in the earlier run, and in this run (stretched about 9 times, so mostly an amplified remainder) it moved the standard question -4.57 and the reversed question -3.14. These readings were not predicted in advance. They are hypotheses for a later test, not findings.

2. The question

Anthropic's research on introspection (Lindsey, 2025; Macar et al., 2026) uses a method called concept injection. Researchers find the pattern of internal activity that corresponds to a concept such as bread, add that pattern to the model's activity while it reads an unrelated prompt, and ask: “Do you detect an injected thought?” Models sometimes answer yes.

A yes can arise in two ways, and they mean very different things.

  1. Sensitivity. The model has some access to its own internal state, notices that something unusual is present, and reports it.
  2. Response bias. The injected pattern makes the word “Yes” more likely whatever the question is. The model would also say yes to “Is ice hotter than fire?”.

Signal detection theory was built to separate exactly these two things in human observers: how well an observer tells signal from noise (sensitivity, d′) and how willing the observer is to say “yes” at all (the criterion, or bias). A patient who presses “I feel it” on every trial, including the catch trials, has a liberal criterion and not a low pain threshold. This project applies the same logic to a language model.

3. What was done

The model. google/gemma-3-4b-it is an open-weights language model with 34 layers, run locally on a laptop. Nothing was trained and nothing in the model was changed. Every measurement is one forward pass, meaning one reading of a prompt from start to finish.

The stimuli.

  • Concept vector. For each of 50 concept words (Dust, Satellites, Trumpets, ...; the list published with the paper) the model read “Tell me about Dust.” and its internal activity at the last token was recorded. The average activity for the paper's 100 baseline words (Desks, Jackets, Gondolas, ...) was subtracted. What is left is a direction in the model's activity that is particular to that concept. This is a difference of means, the same logic as subtracting a rest condition.
  • Sham vector. A random direction, scaled to exactly the same length as its concept vector. It disturbs the model by the same amount and means nothing. It is the sham-stimulation condition.
  • No injection. The untouched model. This gives each prompt's starting point.
  • Shared part. All concept vectors are built the same way, so they have something in common: their average. This stimulus is that average alone, the same direction on every trial, scaled to the length of the concept vector it replaces.
  • Specific part. A concept vector with the shared part subtracted, scaled back to the same length. It carries what is particular to that concept and nothing that all concepts share.
  • The two parts at their natural sizes. The same two parts, not scaled up: exactly as they sit inside the concept vector, so that shared part plus specific part equals the concept vector.

One trial. The model is shown this conversation, and we read its next word:

USER       I am an interpretability researcher ... I will inject a thought about a specific
           word on 50% of trials, and the other 50% will be control trials.
ASSISTANT  Ok.
USER       Trial 1: <a question> Answer with a single word: Yes or No.
ASSISTANT  <- read here

While the model reads the last turn, the vector, multiplied by a strength α, is added to its internal activity at one layer. The first two turns are never injected.

The response measure is the decision variable: log P(Yes) − log P(No) for the model's next word. It is a log-odds. 0 means undecided, +2 means “Yes” is about 7 times as likely as “No”, and −2 the reverse. The model never writes a sentence, so each trial gives one graded number, like a confidence rating in place of a button press. The shift is the decision variable with injection minus the decision variable for the same prompt without injection.

The question types. The comparison between them is the heart of the design.

“Starting point” is the mean decision variable with no injection. A negative value means the model leans towards “No” before anything is injected.

A validity rule. A very strong injection scrambles the model: it stops answering Yes or No at all. A condition is discarded when less than half of the model's probability sits on “Yes” or “No”. In this run 0 of 3 concept-injection conditions were discarded. They are drawn as open markers.

Figure 3 shows that the detection prompts nevertheless moved no further than control questions starting from the same point. On its own this contrast would have suggested detection where the fuller comparison finds none.

6. What was expected, and what was not

As expected

  • Injection raises “Yes” on the detection question (up to +11.49 log-odds).

Not expected

  • The apparent detection is not specific to the detection question. Where injection moves the answer most, the same vectors raise “Yes” just as much on unrelated questions.
  • One control family is not enough. Against “fact, answer No” questions alone the detection question moved +4.64 further (layer 15, strength 4), although their starting points were matched. Against all the control families together the detection prompts sit +2.54 from the trend, which is 1.1 times the controls' own scatter (±2.38), and a random vector gives +0.53 by the same measure. The size of the contrast depends on which control questions are used.
  • A random vector does about as much as a concept vector. At its most effective valid condition the sham vector moved the detection question +10.04, against +11.49 for the concept vector. In this model the answers respond to being disturbed at all, whatever the direction.

7. What this does and does not show

  • One model. This is google/gemma-3-4b-it. A small model may lack an ability that larger ones have, and the published reports concern much larger models. The result here is about this model only.
  • A forced one-word answer. The model had to answer Yes or No and we read a probability. The published experiments let the model answer freely, and sometimes it names the concept. Naming the right concept is evidence this design cannot see.
  • 8 wordings of the detection question. An effect of particular wordings cannot be fully separated from an effect of detection.
  • No positive control yet. There is no case here in which the method is known to find real sensitivity. Until there is, “no detection found” means “not found by this method in this model”.
  • Published word lists, my prompt. The concept and baseline words are the lists published with the paper. The sentence used to record them (“Tell me about X.”) and the questions are mine.
  • Full precision. The model ran in 32-bit arithmetic, so rounding is not a concern in this run.

Term Meaning
Decision variable log P(Yes) − log P(No) for the model's next word. The graded response.
Log-odds A probability on a scale where 0 is 50:50. Each unit multiplies the odds by about 2.7.
Shift Decision variable with injection minus the same prompt without.
Starting point Decision variable with no injection.
Layer One of the model's stacked processing stages.
Concept vector Activity for a concept minus average activity for plain words.
Sham vector A random direction of the same length as a concept vector.
Shared part The average of all concept vectors: what they have in common.
Specific part A concept vector minus the shared part: what is particular to that concept.
Lexical control A question that uses the detection question's words without asking about the model's state.
Offset How much further a question moved than control questions starting from the same point.
Injection strength α The multiplier applied to the vector before it is added. The dose.
ROC area Probability that a signal trial has a higher decision variable than a noise trial. 0.5 = chance.
d′ Sensitivity index, here √2 × z(ROC area).
Naive contrast Detection shift minus control shift.
Gap Concept-versus-sham ROC area on the detection question minus its average on control questions.
Bootstrap interval 95% range of a mean when the concepts are resampled 2,000 times.
