Two ways to read emotion inside a language model: eighteen models

The same agreement, specificity and steering tests, unchanged, on eighteen open-weights models from seven families

4 min read

My emotion-readouts study tested two published ways of reading emotion from a language model's activations on one model, Gemma 3 4B, the way a brain signature of pain is tested: do two instruments that claim to measure the same thing agree, and does a "distress" reading respond to distress or to anything intense? One model cannot tell you what is typical. So I ran the same stimuli, instruments and analysis code, changing nothing but the layer (fixed by the same rule), on eighteen open-weights models from 0.27 to 7.3 billion weights.

Short version. Most of what I found in Gemma holds across all eighteen models. The two published instruments agree only moderately, and intense but unemotional passages produce about half of a distress reading's response to real distress. With the words held fixed, whether a scene is pleasant or unpleasant is still readable in every model. Gemma's best result does not generalise: in most models the distress readings cannot tell a scene in which someone who matters is hurt from the same words with a mannequin. Steering with the distress direction makes a model's answers less pleasant than a random push of the same size in every model on average, but by less than half a point on a 9-point scale.

Full report. Read the report: what was run, a check that the code reproduces the Gemma study, every model at its reference layer in one table, the steering results model by model, and interactive figures.

What was the same for every model

  • The stimuli. The 384 passages of round 1 (eight emotions, neutral, and three kinds of intense but unemotional control: urgent, loud, surprising) and the 256 word-matched passages of round 2, in which eight versions of a scene contain exactly the same words.
  • The instruments. A, emotion vectors; B, emotion-word scores read through the model’s own vocabulary; and C, a trained probe as a yardstick. Each is built on seven topics and tested on the eighth.
  • The layer rule. Two thirds of the way through the model, the rule fixed in advance for Gemma.
  • The steering. Four directions (instrument A’s distress direction, two directions built from word-identical scenes, and a random direction of the same size as a sham), each at four doses, while the model answers twelve neutral prompts. GPT Astra rated all 3,672 answers for pleasantness and intensity, blind to model, direction and dose.

Before anything else, Gemma 3 4B was run again through the new code. Its numbers match the Gemma study’s.

The results

Question Across eighteen models (median) In Gemma
Do the two published instruments agree? (correlation, same emotion) 0.39 (range 0.09 to 0.62) moderately
How much of the distress response do intense but unemotional passages produce? A 47%, B 46%, C 43% about half
With identical words, can pleasant be told from unpleasant? yes, in 18 of 18 models yes
Distress, or the same scene with a mannequin? (chance 50%) B 56%, A 47%, C 42% B 88%
Does the distress direction make answers less pleasant than a random push does? yes, in 18 of 18 on average; by 0.43 points at the top dose yes, in a narrow window

Two things stand out. First, counting words does most of round 1’s decoding (92% on eight emotions without looking at the model), which is why round 2 matters: with the words identical, the meaning is still in the activations of every model. Second, the hardest control fails almost everywhere. The distress readings of A and C rank the mannequin version higher than the real one in most models, so they do not track whether anyone who matters is hurt.

The steering result is consistent but small. The distress direction lowers the rated pleasantness of the answers step by step as the dose rises, and a random push of the same size barely moves it. The difference is clear for a single model in seven of eighteen and never goes the other way. The two directions built from word-identical scenes, one from a bad outcome and one from a good one, are nearly the same direction in every model (as in Gemma), and steer the same way: what they carry is “someone who matters is involved”, not distress.

What it does and does not show

It does not show that these models have no emotion-like representations: meaning that survives identical wording is readable in all of them, and a distress direction has a small causal effect on what they write. It shows that a reading from either published instrument should not be called a measure of a specific emotion in these models without two controls: one that holds the words fixed, and one that removes what is at stake.
