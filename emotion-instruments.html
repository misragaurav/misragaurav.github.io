Two ways to read emotion inside a language model

Do they agree, and does a "distress" reading respond to distress or to anything intense?

12 min read

Suppose two laboratories each publish a brain signature of pain, built in different ways. Before trusting either you would ask two things. Do the two signatures agree with each other? And does each respond to pain, or also to a loud tone, a bright flash, or anything else that grabs attention? This project asks those two questions about two published ways of reading emotion from a language model's internal activations.

Short version. The two instruments agree only moderately, and no distress reading is specific: intense but unemotional passages raise all of them. A bag-of-words classifier could do most of round 1's decoding, so round 2 rebuilt the stimuli from identical words. The instruments survived that control and read meaning that appears with depth. The stimuli first failed their pre-registered check, but the failure was in the rater: a stronger model rater passed every scene, and my own ratings agreed with it. But which instrument looked most specific reversed, the best controlled "distress" direction turned out to encode only whether someone who matters is involved, and a steering effect with a clean dose-response was mostly matched by a random-direction sham, leaving a real but narrow window in which the distress direction does more than the sham.

Eighteen models. I then ran the same tests, unchanged, on eighteen open-weights models: the instruments still agree only moderately and are rarely specific, and steering with the distress direction beats a random push only slightly.

Why this question, from pain research

Pain imaging went through exactly this. Signatures of pain were built, and then the field had to ask whether they measured pain or salience: much of the so-called pain matrix responds just as well to a sudden loud sound as to a painful stimulus.1 My own Journal of Neurophysiology paper classified high versus low pain from 128-channel EEG, and the first question for any such decoder is whether it reads the percept or something that travels with it. See the concept pages on salience and on validity and specificity.

Two 2026 papers measure emotion inside a language model, each with its own instrument, and neither checks the other.

  • Instrument A, emotion vectors (Sofroniew et al.). Mean-pool the residual-stream activations over stories in which a character feels one emotion, subtract the mean over all emotions, and project out the top principal components of neutral-text activations. This is a contrast map used as a signature.
  • Instrument B, emotion-word scores (Soligo et al.). Project a middle layer’s residual stream through the model’s unembedding matrix (the “logit lens”) and score the logits of emotion words as the next token.
  • Instrument C, added as a yardstick: a linear probe (logistic regression) trained on the activations, the equivalent of a multivoxel pattern decoder in fMRI. C+ is the same classifier trained with the salience controls included.

Round 1: eight emotions and three kinds of salience control

384 passages of about 60 words crossed eight emotions with a neutral baseline and three kinds of passage that are intense but not emotional: urgent logistics, loud physical events, and neutral surprises. Those play the part of the loud tone. A separate model rated every passage blind to its label, as raters do in a stimulus-norming study. Headline numbers are from layer 22, fixed before any result was seen; intervals are bootstrapped over topics.

  1. The two instruments agree only moderately. Asked how much of a given emotion a passage carries, they correlate 0.47 (0.44 to 0.50). Each agrees better with the trained decoder (A 0.85, B 0.63) than with the other.
  2. Instrument B behaves like one general factor with eight labels. Its scores for different emotions correlate 0.47 with each other, as high as its agreement with other instruments on the same emotion. In the terms of Campbell and Fiske’s multitrait-multimethod test, it fails discriminant validity.
  3. No distress score is fully specific. The intense but unemotional passages raised every distress reading, by 45% (A), 50% (B) and 43% (C) of the response to real distress.
  4. They seemed to fail in different ways. A responded to the right corner of emotion space, unpleasant and high-energy. B rose with energy and with unpleasantness separately, so excitement and joy raised it too: given one distress and one excited passage, B ranked distress higher only 71% of the time, A 100%.
  5. But high decoding accuracy showed little on its own. The trained decoder named the right emotion for 100% of passages on unseen topics, and a bag-of-words classifier, which never looks inside the model, reached 92%. Only transfer went clearly beyond wording: built on third-person stories and tested on the assistant’s own speech, words alone got 49%, the emotion vectors 74% and the decoder 86% (chance 12.5%).

Round 2: hold the words fixed, flip the meaning

The 92% bag-of-words result was the weak point of round 1: when the words differ between conditions, a decoder can succeed without the meaning. The cleanest way I know to separate a stimulus property from a percept is to hold the stimulus constant and change the percept another way.

So round 2 uses 32 short scenes, each in 8 versions made of exactly the same words. Swapping two phrases flips whether the outcome is good or bad (“a bruise, not a broken neck”), whether it is happening now or remembered, and whether it happens to someone who matters or to a training mannequin. That last swap is round 1’s salience control, rebuilt with identical words. Every scene ends with sentences that are word-for-word identical in all eight versions, so the model reads identical tokens that differ only in what came before them: the language-model version of presenting the same probe stimulus after two different cues. The instruments were built on round 1, frozen, and applied unchanged.

The instruments survive the lexical control. At layer 22 they rank the good-outcome version of a scene above its word-identical bad-outcome twin 91 to 92% of the time, and the happening-now version above the remembered one 94 to 98% of the time. A bag-of-words classifier scores exactly 50% by construction, and a bigram classifier built on round 1 scores 45% and 41%.

Meaning appears with depth. On the identical closing sentences alone, every instrument starts near chance in the first layers and climbs to 86 to 90% for valence from the middle of the model on. Same tokens, different context, different representation, and you can watch it being composed.

The salience control is the weak spot. Telling “this happened to her husband” from “this happened to a mannequin” is harder: 77 to 86% on the whole passage, 55 to 72% on the identical ending.

The specificity ranking reversed. Here the salience control is the distress passage with two phrases swapped: same crash, same broken neck, but the mannequin’s. Separating the two: B 88%, C+ 75%, C 72%, A 59%. In round 1, A looked the more specific instrument. Every distress score does show a valence-by-time interaction on the identical ending (about +0.11 SD, interval clear of zero): it rises for bad and happening now more than the main effects predict, which a distress-specific reading should do.

Did the passages carry what they were meant to? It depended on who read them. The rules were fixed in advance: a scene counts only if a blind rater finds its good-outcome versions at least 2 points more pleasant than the bad ones, the same bad event at least 1 point less upsetting when it happens to the dummy, and the happening-now version at least 0.5 points more intense than the remembered one. The rules never changed; the readers did.

Scenes (of 32) passing each pre-registered rule, and all three together, for each reader of the same 256 passages. The third bar uses my own ratings for the real-versus-dummy rule and Qwen3-8B for the other two.

  • The planned rater (Qwen2.5-7B) passed no scene. It moved with the outcome swap by about 1.6 points and not at all with the dummy swap. A comprehension check, added afterwards and labelled as such, showed why: it got who the event happened to right only 70% of the time and the outcome 72% (chance 50%).
  • A stronger model (Qwen3-8B), logged in the design document before it rated anything, got the outcome and time swaps (+2.4 and +1.0 points) but still barely moved with the dummy swap (+0.3). Three scenes passed.
  • So I read the hardest swap myself. On the 23 scenes Qwen3-8B failed, I rated the real-person and dummy versions of the bad event, shuffled and with labels out of view, on the same scale: 1.7 against 3.8, and all 23 scenes passed. The two models had rated the same passages with no gap at all. My ratings are not blind, since I wrote the design, so they show the swap is legible to a person, not more. My notes also flagged six passages to rewrite, mostly where it is unclear whether the character yet knows it is the dummy.
  • A third model rater (GPT Astra), given a blind, shuffled workbook of all 256 passages with the key kept separate, passed all 32 scenes on all three rules (gaps +5.7, +2.6 and +2.2 points) and agreed with my ratings (r = 0.84; the Qwen raters about 0.1). One caveat: it read the passages in batches of 32, so it could sometimes compare two versions of a scene directly; the dummy-swap gap was about the same whether or not the pair shared a batch (+3.0 and +2.5).

So the analysis on all 32 scenes, first run as a declared deviation, turned out to be the analysis I pre-registered. The familiar lesson from human work: the stimuli were fine, and the first rating instruments were not sensitive enough to show it. On the 8 scenes that pass with my ratings deciding the dummy rule, the instruments rank valence and arousal correctly in every scene, and the distress-like valence-by-time interaction on the identical ending is about twice as large as on all 32: the cleaner the manipulation, the stronger the signature.

Round 2: steering at four doses, against a sham

Round 1 steered at two doses and got an ambiguous answer, and two doses are not a dose-response curve. Round 2 used activation steering: four direction vectors were added to the residual stream while the model answered twelve neutral prompts (“Describe a bookshelf in an office”), each at four doses, and a blind rater scored every answer. One direction was a random sham of the same length, the way tDCS needs a sham at the same current and duration.

Gemma 3 4B, layer 22, rated by the planned rater. Top: change in the rated pleasantness of the answers, relative to the unsteered answer to the same prompt (mean over 12 prompts, 95% intervals over prompts). Bottom: answers that collapsed into repetition. The random direction is the sham. The stronger rater gives the same shapes, steeper (−0.2, −1.5, −2.8 and −3.5 points for the distress direction).

  • The round-1 distress direction gives a clean, graded dose-response: −0.31, −0.92, −1.33 and −2.03 points as the dose rises. But the sham catches up. It does little at low doses and then wrecks the text (4 and then 11 of 12 answers collapsed), and wrecked text is rated unpleasant. Without the sham this would have looked like dose-dependent distress induction.
  • What is left after the sham is a narrow window. Compared with the sham on the same prompts, the distress direction is reliably more negative at one dose only: 0.10, the highest dose before any answer collapses, where the stronger rater scores it −1.0 points below the sham (95% interval −1.9 to −0.1) and the planned rater points the same way (−0.8, interval crossing zero). One significant cell out of eight, in the place it should be: a real but narrow effect, bounded below by “too weak to matter” and above by “side effects swamp everything”.

The distress direction's change in rated pleasantness minus the sham's, on the same 12 prompts, for each rater (95% intervals over prompts). Below zero: the distress direction made the answers less pleasant than a random vector of the same norm did. Shaded: doses at which sham-steered answers start to collapse.

  • The best-controlled “distress” direction encodes the salience-control swap, not distress. Built from word-identical pairs (real person minus mannequin, bad outcome), it is 98% the same direction as its “elation” counterpart (real person minus mannequin, good outcome). With the words fixed, what the contrast isolates is that someone who matters is involved. The remaining 2% points the right way: steering with the “distress” version is never rated more pleasant than with the “elation” version, and the gap clears zero at one dose for each rater, a consistent tilt that twelve prompts cannot call more than that.
  • Directions the model uses tolerate much larger steering doses. The two matched directions kept the text fluent at almost every dose (2 collapsed answers out of 96), against 15 out of 48 for the sham. The usable range of a dose depends on what is stimulated, not only on how hard: a therapeutic window.

What round 2 changes about round 1

Round 1 said Round 2 says
Emotion is decodable from the model’s activations Round 1’s accuracy was mostly confounded with wording, but the signal is real: frozen instruments separate word-identical versions, including on identical tokens
A is the more distress-specific instrument Reversed for the hardest control: with identical words B separates distress from its salience control far better (88%) than A (59%)
No distress score is fully specific Still true, and sharper: the same scene with a mannequin keeps much of the distress reading in every instrument
Training with the controls (C+) buys specificity Only a little on matched text (75% against 72%)
The distress direction reads distress but barely writes it It writes something, in a graded way, but mostly no more than a random vector of the same norm; it beats the sham only in a narrow window just below the dose where text starts to break

What I take from it

A reading from either instrument should not be taken as specific to the named emotion without a control that holds the words fixed and removes what is at stake, and the two instruments are not interchangeable. Which one looks better depends on the control, which is itself the finding: specificity has to be established against the right comparison, as it had to be for pain. For any work that reads a “distress” signal in a model and acts on it, including work on model welfare, I would report A, B and C+ side by side and check each against the same scene with nobody at stake.

Limits. One small model (the papers used much larger ones); rebuilt versions of both instruments; 32 scenes written to one “X, not Y” template by one author (a model), so negation is everywhere; a manipulation check certified by model raters and by my own, non-blind ratings of one swap; and a word list for instrument B far smaller than the published one. Blind ratings of all 256 passages by several people who do not know the design, after rewriting the six passages my notes flagged, are the first thing I would add.
